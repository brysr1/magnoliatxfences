<p><strong>Look.</strong> Wood is the fence most people picture when they think of a backyard, and for good reason. Cedar has a warm, reddish tone that settles into a soft silver if you leave it alone, while pressure-treated pine is a little more utilitarian and takes stain well. Both can be built side-by-side, board-on-board for full privacy, or shadowbox so both neighbors get a finished face. Add a top cap and trim board and a plain privacy fence starts to look custom.</p>

<p><strong>Upkeep.</strong> In our climate, wood needs a little attention to reach its full life. We recommend a penetrating stain or sealer within the first few months, then every few years after that, to slow moisture damage and graying. Keeping sprinklers off the boards and leaves off the bottom rail helps too. Individual pickets are easy and inexpensive to replace if one gets damaged.</p>

<p><strong>Cost.</strong> Wood is a mid-range choice &mdash; more than chain link, generally less than vinyl, composite, or ornamental metal. Pine is the budget end; cedar costs more up front but resists rot and insects better on its own. Post choice, fence height, and style (board-on-board uses more lumber) all move the price, and we spell each of those out in your written quote.</p>

<h2>How wood and cedar holds up in the Magnolia area</h2>
<p class="section-intro76">Wood is the most common fence in the Magnolia area, and it can last a long time here &mdash; as long as it's built and cared for with our climate in mind.</p>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li><strong>Sun and heat.</strong> Long Texas summers gray untreated wood within a season or two and dry it out until boards check and cup. A penetrating stain with UV protection slows both.</li>
<li><strong>Humidity and rain.</strong> Moisture is what shortens a wood fence's life here. A rot board along the bottom keeps pickets off the ground, and resealing every few years keeps water out of the grain.</li>
<li><strong>Sandy and clay soils.</strong> Montgomery County soil ranges from loose sand to heavy clay that swells when wet and shrinks in a drought. We set post depth and backfill for the soil on your lot so the fence stays plumb, and we request Texas 811 locates before any digging.</li>
<li><strong>Storm wind.</strong> A solid wood privacy fence catches wind like a sail. Well-set posts, sturdy rails, and quality fasteners are what help it ride out Gulf storms.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Acreage or subdivision lot?</h2>
<p class="section-intro76">Properties around Magnolia and Pinehurst range from tight subdivision lots to multi-acre tracts in unincorporated Montgomery County. Here's where wood and cedar fits best.</p>
<div class="cards76">
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>On acreage</h3><p>On acreage, wood works best close to the house &mdash; around a backyard, pool, or patio &mdash; where privacy matters most. Across a full property line, long runs of wood privacy fencing get expensive and add upkeep, so many owners pair a wood section near the home with ranch fencing beyond it. Wood rail along road frontage is the popular exception.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>On subdivision lots</h3><p>On subdivision lots, wood privacy fencing is the style we install most, and it's the standard fence in many deed-restricted neighborhoods. If your property has HOA or deed restrictions, check with your board on height, style, and stain color before you choose.</p></div></div>
