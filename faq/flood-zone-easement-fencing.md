<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> In many cases, yes, but the answer depends on three separate things: the FEMA flood zone, any easement on your plat, and, on a lake lot, the SJRA boundary. Each can change the permit, the fence style, or whether a fence belongs there at all.</p>
</div>
<h2>Check the map and the plat first</h2>
<p>Flood zones and easements get mixed up, but they come from different places. Your flood zone is on FEMA's flood maps, which you can look up by address at the <a href="https://msc.fema.gov/" rel="noopener">FEMA Map Service Center</a>. Easements are on your recorded plat and your survey, shown as dashed strips along the back or side lot lines labeled for drainage or utilities.</p>
<p>Around Magnolia, both show up most on acreage that backs to a creek or a low draw, and on lots near the Spring Creek and Lake Creek drainages. In Harvey, SJRA reported more than 21 inches of rain in the Lake Creek watershed and more than 29 inches in parts of the Spring Creek watershed, so those lines on the map aren't hypothetical.</p>
<h2>Montgomery County's floodplain permit</h2>
<p>The county's floodplain management regulations define development broadly, including excavation and drilling, and require a development permit for it inside the mapped 100-year floodplain. They exempt one thing by name: a three-strand barbed wire fence. That suggests a wood privacy fence or a no-climb run inside the floodplain may need a permit. The county's FAQ doesn't spell out fences, so we confirm with the county floodplain office before building. Inside city limits, the city's own rules apply as well; see <a href="/faq/fence-permits-magnolia-montgomery/">fence permits around Magnolia and Montgomery</a>.</p>
<h2>Lake Conroe lots</h2>
<p>On the west shore, the SJRA's 201-foot contour marks the edge of Authority Land, and the SJRA also refers to a flowage easement up to 207 feet that operators try to keep the lake below. News reports citing officials put the Harvey peak at roughly 206 feet, and the lake reached about 205 feet in the May 2024 flooding. A fence low on a Bentwater or Walden lot can sit under water in a big release, so we plan the low end as open fencing or stop the run short of the slope.</p>
<h2>Easements: access and flow</h2>
<p>A drainage easement exists so water, and the crews who maintain the ditch, can get through. A utility easement holds lines that may need digging up. Deed restrictions or the easement holder can limit what goes there. If a fence must cross one, plan for a removable section or a gate wide enough for equipment.</p>
<h2>Fences that let water pass</h2>
<ul>
<li>Ornamental aluminum or steel, which leaves most of the face open</li>
<li>Split rail or ranch rail, the classic choice along a creek bottom</li>
<li>Wire fencing with a water gap where it crosses a creek or ditch</li>
<li>Wood privacy raised a few inches off grade, so sheet flow isn't dammed against the boards</li>
</ul>
<p>Solid privacy panels catch debris and push water sideways into a neighbor's yard, which is why we steer away from them across a drainage path. Our <a href="/materials/farm-ranch/">farm and ranch fencing</a> and <a href="/materials/ornamental-steel-aluminum/">ornamental metal</a> pages show the open styles in more detail.</p>
