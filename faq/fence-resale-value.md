<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> A fence adds value mostly by meeting what buyers in your area already expect, and by being in good shape. We don't quote return-on-investment figures, because a fence's effect on price depends on the market, the house, and the neighborhood. A leaning, gray, or out-of-compliance fence, though, is something buyers and their paperwork tend to surface.</p>
</div>
<h2>Where a fence shows up in a Texas sale</h2>
<p>Before talking about looks, it helps to know where a fence actually appears in the paperwork when a house sells here.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The seller's disclosure.</strong> The statutory notice in <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/htm/PR.5.htm#5.008" rel="noopener">Texas Property Code Section 5.008</a> lists fences among the items a seller marks, and it asks about alterations or repairs made without necessary permits and about notices of deed-restriction violations. A fence built inside the City of Magnolia without its permit, or one that drew a letter from the HOA, can turn into a disclosure question.</li>
<li><strong>The HOA resale certificate.</strong> Under <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PR/htm/PR.207.htm#207.003" rel="noopener">Section 207.003</a>, the association's certificate must describe conditions on the lot that the board knows violate the subdivision's restrictions. An unapproved fence color, height, or style is exactly that kind of condition.</li>
<li><strong>The inspection.</strong> TREC's <a href="https://www.trec.texas.gov/online-sops" rel="noopener">standards of practice</a> don't require an inspector to look at fences (Rule 535.227), but an optional pool inspection reports missing or deficient safety barriers (Rule 535.233). On a pool lot, the barrier and its gates get looked at.</li>
</ul>
<p>None of that is legal advice; your agent handles the forms. The practical point is that approvals and permits are worth having in a folder before you list. Our <a href="/faq/fence-permits-magnolia-montgomery/">permit FAQ</a> covers which ones apply to your address.</p>
<h2>Fitting what the neighborhood calls for</h2>
<p>Around Magnolia, Pinehurst, and Montgomery, "the right fence" depends on the kind of property.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Subdivision homes.</strong> In Audubon, North Grove, Woodtrace, and similar neighborhoods, the HOA guidelines already define the fence: height, style, stain color, and where the side fence stops. A fence that matches them and the fences on either side is the baseline. Vinyl or composite can appeal to buyers who don't want to stain, if the HOA allows it.</li>
<li><strong>Acreage.</strong> On tracts in High Meadow Estates, Lake Windcrest, or off FM 1774, a buyer with animals will look at what the fence can actually hold: tight wire, braced corners that haven't tipped, and gates wide enough for a trailer. Some of those communities have POA rules on fencing too.</li>
<li><strong>Lake and golf lots.</strong> In Bentwater, April Sound, and Walden, the architectural committees usually favor open ornamental aluminum or steel toward the water or fairway, since the view is shared. A fence that sits below SJRA's 201-foot contour without a permit is a question you don't want raised during a sale; see <a href="/faq/property-survey/">property surveys</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Condition matters more than material</h2>
<p>A well-kept pine fence will show better than a neglected cedar one. Before you list, look at the fence the way a buyer would:</p>
<ul>
<li>Do the posts stand straight, and do the gates open and latch without lifting?</li>
<li>Are there missing, broken, or rotted pickets, especially along the bottom where pine straw piles up?</li>
<li>Is the stain even, or is the fence gray and splotchy?</li>
<li>Does it match the HOA's approved height, style, and color?</li>
<li>On a pool lot, do the gates still self-close and self-latch?</li>
</ul>
<p>Our page on <a href="/faq/how-long-does-a-fence-last/">fence lifespan</a> can help you judge if yours has years left or is near the end.</p>
<h2>Before you list: fix, refinish, or replace?</h2>
<p>If the structure is sound and the wood is just weathered, a cleaning and fresh stain is the most for the least money. Our <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining crew</a> can match an HOA-approved color. If a couple of posts lean or a gate scrapes the ground, a targeted <a href="/maintenance-repair/">repair</a> fixes what a buyer will notice first.</p>
<p>A full replacement makes sense when most of the posts along the line are loose or rotting, or when the fence doesn't fit the neighborhood at all. Typical installed ranges are about $25 to $35 a foot for pressure-treated pine, $30 to $47 for cedar, and $40 to $100 for aluminum or steel, per our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>; tear-out of the old fence is quoted separately where it applies. If you're replacing close to a sale, build what the HOA already approves and get that approval in writing; see our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing</a> page. A free estimate gives you a written number to weigh against your listing plans.</p>
