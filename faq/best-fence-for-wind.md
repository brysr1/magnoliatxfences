<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Ornamental aluminum or steel, chain link, and ranch wire hold up best because wind passes straight through them. If you need privacy, a shadowbox or other semi-private wood fence takes less load than a solid one, and any privacy fence does far better with deep posts, tight spacing, and bracketed rails.</p>
</div>
<h2>Why a solid fence acts like a sail</h2>
<p>A 6-foot board fence presents a solid face to the wind along its whole length. All that push travels through the rails into the posts and down to the footing, and the post bends or rocks at the ground line. In sandy topsoil that rocking slowly widens the hole, which is why a privacy fence that survives one storm can lean after the next.</p>
<p>Hurricane Beryl showed what our area can see. The National Weather Service's preliminary <a href="https://www.weather.gov/hgx/beryl2024" rel="noopener">Beryl summary</a> lists a peak gust of 69 mph at the Conroe weather station on July 8, 2024. Gusts in that range will find any weak post on a long privacy line.</p>
<h2>Fence types ranked for windy spots</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Field wire, barbed wire, and no-climb mesh.</strong> Almost no wind load. On acreage, falling limbs are the bigger risk to these lines.</li>
<li><strong>Chain link.</strong> Wind passes through, as long as you skip privacy slats in exposed spots.</li>
<li><strong>Ornamental aluminum or steel.</strong> Open pickets with very little surface area. Steel takes a blow from debris better than aluminum.</li>
<li><strong>Semi-privacy wood or vinyl.</strong> Shadowbox, spaced boards, and lattice tops let some air through. See <a href="/fence-styles/semi-privacy/">semi-privacy fences</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Solid privacy in wood, vinyl, or composite.</strong> The most wind load, so build quality matters most here.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Exposed lots and sheltered lots</h2>
<p>Where you live changes the risk. An open pasture tract west of Magnolia has nothing to slow the wind, so a privacy fence around the house takes the full force of a gust. A wooded lot in Pinehurst is sheltered from steady wind but has trees that can drop limbs or come down across the fence entirely.</p>
<p>Waterfront lots in Bentwater, Walden, and Grand Harbor catch wind that has crossed open water. That's one more reason open ornamental fencing makes sense on the lake side, with privacy sections kept to the spaces between homes.</p>
<h2>Build details that matter when the wind picks up</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Post depth.</strong> Every post goes in concrete, at least 24 inches deep with 80 lb per post, deeper in loose sand or wet holes. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting posts in local soil</a> covers how we decide.</li>
<li><strong>Corners, ends, and gates.</strong> They're dug deeper than line posts since they take the most strain, and a gate swinging in a gust adds leverage on top of the wind.</li>
<li><strong>Rail connections.</strong> Our wood privacy fences get three rails, each fastened to the posts with Simpson brackets rather than toe-nailed.</li>
<li><strong>Fasteners.</strong> Hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails hold pickets on when the fence flexes.</li>
</ul>
