<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Ornamental aluminum or steel, chain link, and ranch wire hold up best because wind passes straight through them. If you need privacy, a shadowbox or other semi-private wood fence takes less load than a solid one, and any privacy fence does far better with deep posts, tight spacing, and bracketed rails.</p>
</div>
<h2>What the wind has done here</h2>
<p>Two storms set the bar for our area. During Hurricane Ike in September 2008, the National Hurricane Center's <a href="https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL092008_Ike.pdf" rel="noopener">Ike report</a> recorded a 52-knot gust, about 60 mph, at the Conroe airport. During Beryl in July 2024, the National Weather Service's preliminary <a href="https://www.weather.gov/hgx/beryl2024" rel="noopener">Beryl summary</a> lists a 69 mph gust at the same station. On that same morning, a citizen weather station in Magnolia logged 39 mph. Those readings are unofficial, but they show how much gusts vary from one spot to the next. A fence a few miles away can see a very different storm.</p>
<h2>Why a solid fence acts like a sail</h2>
<p>A 6-foot board fence presents a solid face to the wind along its whole length. All that push travels through the rails into the posts and down to the footing, and the post bends or rocks at the ground line. In sandy topsoil that rocking slowly widens the hole, which is why a privacy fence that survives one storm can lean after the next.</p>
<p>The soil adds a wrinkle. The USDA's <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/C/CONROE.html" rel="noopener">Conroe soil series description</a>, whose type location is in Montgomery County, puts the start of the plinthite (ironstone) layer at roughly 23 to 43 inches down, right around the depth of a fence post. Water drains slowly there. After days of rain, the sand around a post is wet and soft just when a tropical system brings its strongest gusts.</p>
<h2>Fence types ranked for windy spots</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Field wire, barbed wire, and no-climb mesh.</strong> Almost no wind load. On acreage, falling limbs are the bigger risk to these lines.</li>
<li><strong>Chain link.</strong> Wind passes through, as long as you skip privacy slats in exposed spots. Installed, it runs $18&ndash;$40 a foot on our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Ornamental aluminum or steel.</strong> Open pickets with very little surface area, both at $40&ndash;$100 a foot. Steel takes a blow from debris better. Aluminum won't rust, though it can pit over time. See <a href="/materials/ornamental-steel-aluminum/">ornamental steel and aluminum</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Semi-privacy wood or vinyl.</strong> Shadowbox, spaced boards, and lattice tops let some air through. See <a href="/fence-styles/semi-privacy/">semi-privacy fences</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Solid privacy in wood, vinyl, or composite.</strong> The most wind load, so build quality matters most here.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Exposed lots, sheltered lots, and HOA limits</h2>
<p>An open pasture tract west of Magnolia has nothing to slow the wind, so a privacy fence around the house takes the full force of a gust. A wooded lot in Pinehurst is sheltered from steady wind but has trees that can drop limbs or come down across the fence entirely. Waterfront lots in Bentwater, Walden, and Grand Harbor catch wind that has crossed open water, which is one more reason open ornamental fencing makes sense on the lake side.</p>
<p>Deed restrictions shape the answer too. <a href="/neighborhoods/ranch-crest/">Ranch Crest's</a> newer sections allow fences up to 8 feet, and an 8-foot solid fence needs a heavier post plan than a 6-foot one. <a href="/neighborhoods/walden-on-lake-conroe/">Walden's</a> proposed Section 4 rules would limit waterfront fences behind the house to 4-foot open metal, which happens to be one of the most wind-tolerant fences you can build. <a href="/neighborhoods/westwood/">Westwood's</a> 2016 guidelines call for posts at least 12 inches deep in concrete. That's a minimum for the neighborhood. Ours go at least twice that.</p>
<h2>Build details that matter when the wind picks up</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Post depth.</strong> Every wood-fence post goes in concrete, at least 24 inches deep with 80 lb per post, deeper for taller fences, in loose sand, or in wet holes. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting posts in local soil</a> covers how we decide.</li>
<li><strong>Corners, ends, and gates.</strong> They're dug deeper than line posts, sized to the gate and the soil. A solid drive gate catches wind like a privacy panel and carries it on its hinges, so on acreage entrances a slide gate or a split pair of leaves can be easier on the posts.</li>
<li><strong>Rail connections.</strong> Our wood privacy fences get three rails, each fastened to the posts with Simpson brackets rather than toe-nailed.</li>
<li><strong>Fasteners.</strong> Hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails hold pickets on when the fence flexes.</li>
</ul>
