<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Whoever wants the fence usually pays for it, unless both owners agree to share. Texas has no statewide rule dividing the bill, but it does limit tearing out a fence two owners share. This page is general information, not legal advice.</p>
</div>
<h2>A split is a private deal</h2>
<p>Texas A&amp;M AgriLife Extension's <a href="https://agecoext.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2019/06/Overview-of-Texas-Fencing-Laws.pdf" rel="noopener">overview of Texas fencing laws</a> puts it simply: neighbors don't have to share the cost of building, maintaining, or replacing a boundary fence unless they agree to. If one owner pays for all of it, that owner owns it, and AgriLife suggests keeping proof that you paid.</p>
<p>Around Magnolia, the split most often comes up in two situations. In newer subdivisions like Audubon, many homes still have the builder fence, and when one panel between two backyards starts to lean, both neighbors are looking at the same first replacement. On acreage, a new owner wants to replace a sagging wire fence with the neighbor's pasture on the other side. In both cases nothing in state law sets the shares. Neighbors agree to 50/50, to one paying for materials and the other for labor, or to one owner covering it all and keeping control of the style.</p>
<p>Deed restrictions can add rules about who may build on a lot line, what it looks like, and which side faces out. In <a href="/neighborhoods/ranch-crest/">Ranch Crest's</a> newer sections, for example, fences need ACC approval and street-facing fences must line up uniformly. Our <a href="/resources/hoa-fence-approval-magnolia-tx/">HOA approval guide</a> notes that on a shared fence, both owners may need to sign the application.</p>
<h2>The shared-fence rules in Ag Code 143</h2>
<p>Chapter 143 of the Texas Agriculture Code doesn't make anyone contribute, but it does limit taking a shared fence down. Under <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/AG/htm/AG.143.htm#143.121" rel="noopener">Section 143.121</a>, a person may not remove a dividing fence they own jointly, or a fence attached to someone else's, except as the subchapter allows or by mutual consent. Sections 143.122 and 143.123 set out the way to separate attached fences, which runs through six months' written notice.</p>
<p>On acreage that matters more than it does between two backyards. In horse communities like <a href="/neighborhoods/indigo-lake-estates/">Indigo Lake Estates</a>, where deed restrictions allow horses, the neighbor's animals may depend on that wire. Pulling the old fence on a Saturday to rebuild it can leave the next pasture open.</p>
<h2>Livestock changes who cares most</h2>
<p>Texas starts from open range, where livestock owners have no general duty to fence animals in. Counties can change that with local stock laws adopted by election, and AgriLife notes those laws can be hard to track down because they're often recorded only in old commissioners court minutes. The highway rule in Ag Code 143.102 covers U.S. and state highways but, according to AgriLife, not numbered farm-to-market roads like FM 1488 or FM 1774. The practical result is the same either way: the owner with animals usually wants the tightest boundary fence, and is often the one most willing to pay for it.</p>
<h2>Write the agreement down</h2>
<p>A short signed note avoids most of the arguments we hear about later. Cover:</p>
<ul>
<li>Each owner's share, and when each payment is due</li>
<li>Material, height, style, and which side gets the finished face</li>
<li>Exactly where the line runs, ideally tied to pins or a <a href="/faq/property-survey/">survey</a></li>
<li>Who maintains, stains, and repairs it, and how storm damage is split</li>
<li>What happens if one house sells</li>
</ul>
<p>On our side, we can invoice each neighbor separately, or break out the shared run on one quote so each of you sees the number you're splitting.</p>
<h2>Building a few inches inside your line</h2>
<p>Some owners skip the negotiation and set the fence slightly inside their own boundary. The fence is then yours outright, and you choose the design. Leaving a narrow strip outside the fence doesn't by itself give it away. Texas courts presume an old fence of unknown purpose is a "casual fence," which generally doesn't support an adverse possession claim, as AgriLife explains in its write-up of a <a href="https://agrilife.org/texasaglaw/2024/07/01/fence-boundary-dispute-involves-adverse-possession-claim/" rel="noopener">2024 fence boundary case</a>. Adverse possession has its own conditions, so a real estate attorney is the right call if a strip is in dispute.</p>
<p>If you're paying for the full run yourself, our <a href="/financing/">financing options</a> can spread out the cost, and the <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a> gives typical per-foot ranges for each material: for example, $25&ndash;$35 a foot for pressure-treated pine and $30&ndash;$47 for cedar.</p>
