<div class="callout76">
<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Around Magnolia, most posts fail because they were set too shallow for our sandy topsoil, set in a hole that held water, or rotted at the ground line. Tree roots, concrete that traps rain against the wood, and the pull of a heavy gate account for most of the rest. How the post was set matters more than what the fence is made of.</p>
</div>

<h2>What's two feet under a Magnolia yard</h2>
<p>The most common soil in Montgomery County is Conroe loamy fine sand, and its official <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/C/CONROE.html" rel="noopener">USDA series description</a> was written from a site near Willis, in this county. It reads like a description of a post hole:</p>
<ul>
<li>Loamy fine sand from the surface down to the clay layer, which starts about 20 to 33 inches deep.</li>
<li>Below that, sandy clay loam and sandy clay with plinthite, the reddish iron-rich layer most people call ironstone, starting about 23 to 43 inches down.</li>
<li>Slow permeability, with drainage slowing further inside the plinthite.</li>
</ul>
<p>Put a 24-inch post hole in that profile and the bottom often lands right at the line between sand and clay. That's the spot where rainwater stops and sits. The clay itself is kaolinitic, a type that doesn't swell and shrink much, so heaving clay isn't usually the culprit here the way it is closer to Houston. Sand that won't grip and water that won't drain are.</p>

<h2>The usual causes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Too shallow for sand.</strong> An auger goes through the top layer fast, but loose sand holds a post loosely. A post with a short hole and a skimpy footing can stand for a few years and then tip after a wet spring and a windy afternoon.</li>
<li><strong>Water in the hole.</strong> Because the clay drains slowly, a fresh hole on a low lot can partly fill after rain or even while we dig. Concrete poured into standing water cures weak and can break up around the post.</li>
<li><strong>Stopping at the ironstone.</strong> Plinthite is hard enough to stall an auger. A crew that quits there leaves the post shallower than it should be.</li>
<li><strong>Rot at the soil line.</strong> Wood breaks down fastest where it's damp and has air, right at grade. Mulch beds, pine straw, sprinkler heads aimed at the fence, and soil built up against the boards all speed it along. The post looks fine from the yard until a storm snaps it at the ground.</li>
<li><strong>Concrete that holds water.</strong> When the top of the footing sits below grade or forms a cup around the post, rain pools against the wood instead of running off.</li>
<li><strong>Roots and trees.</strong> Pines and oaks along a fence line shove footings as their roots grow, and a falling limb can crack a post that was otherwise sound.</li>
<li><strong>Gate leverage.</strong> A gate hangs its full weight off one post and jolts it with every slam, so gate posts tend to go first.</li>
<li><strong>Loose fill on new lots.</strong> Newer lots built up with fill can be looser than the native ground beside them.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How to tell which one you have</h2>
<p>Grab the post near the top and push. If the post moves inside its concrete, the footing was too small or has broken up. If the post and concrete rock together as one block, the footing isn't deep or wide enough for the sand around it. If the post bends right at the ground, press a screwdriver into the wood at the soil line. If it sinks in easily, that's rot, and the post needs replacing rather than straightening. Several posts leaning the same way along a run point to how the whole fence was built, not to one bad spot.</p>
<p>On a wire fence across pasture, check the corner and H-brace assemblies before the line posts. A brace that has tipped lets the whole run go slack.</p>

<h2>Fixing it, and building it right the first time</h2>
<p>A single leaning line post can often be reset, while a rotted post needs to be replaced. The <a href="/resources/fix-leaning-fence-post-sandy-soil/">leaning post repair guide</a> covers when a brace is enough and when it isn't.</p>
<p>When we build a new fence, all posts are concreted in. For wood fences that means a minimum 24-inch hole and 80 pounds of concrete per post. We treat that as the floor: taller fences, loose or wet sand, and gate and corner posts get more depth and width, sized to the gate and what we find in the hole. Standing water comes out before concrete goes in, we work through ironstone rather than stopping on it, and every wood fence gets a 6-inch rot board unless you'd rather skip it. Galvanized steel posts behind wood pickets are an option where rot at grade is the main worry.
