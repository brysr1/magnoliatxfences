<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Around Magnolia, most posts fail because they were set too shallow for our sandy topsoil, set in a hole that held water, or rotted at the ground line. Tree roots, concrete that traps rain against the wood, and the pull of a heavy gate account for most of the rest. How the post was set matters more than what the fence is made of.</p>
</div>

<h2>The usual causes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Too shallow for sand.</strong> The top layer on most Montgomery County lots is loamy sand. An auger goes through it fast, but it holds a post loosely, so one with a short hole and a skimpy footing can stand for a few years and then tip after a wet spring and a windy afternoon.</li>
<li><strong>Water in the hole.</strong> The clay under the sand drains slowly. After rain, a fresh hole can hold water, and concrete poured into it cures weak and can break up around the post.</li>
<li><strong>Rot at the soil line.</strong> Wood breaks down fastest where it's damp and has air, which is right at grade. Mulch beds, sprinkler heads aimed at the fence, and soil built up against the boards all speed it along. The post looks fine from the yard until a storm snaps it at the ground.</li>
<li><strong>Concrete that holds water.</strong> When the top of the footing sits below grade or forms a cup around the post, rain pools against the wood instead of running off.</li>
<li><strong>Roots and trees.</strong> Pines and oaks along a fence line shove footings as their roots grow, and a falling limb can crack a post that was otherwise sound.</li>
<li><strong>Gate leverage.</strong> A gate hangs its full weight off one post and jolts it with every slam, so gate posts tend to go first.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How to tell which one you have</h2>
<p>Grab the post near the top and push. If the post moves inside its concrete, the footing was too small or has broken up. If the post and concrete rock together as one block, the footing isn't deep or wide enough for the sand around it. If the post bends right at the ground, press a screwdriver into the wood at the soil line. If it sinks in easily, that's rot, and the post needs replacing rather than straightening.</p>

<h2>Fixing it, and building it right the first time</h2>
<p>A single leaning line post can often be reset, while a rotted post needs to be replaced. The <a href="/resources/fix-leaning-fence-post-sandy-soil/">leaning post repair guide</a> covers when a brace is enough and when it isn't.</p>
<p>When we build a new fence, all posts are concreted in. For wood fences that means a minimum 24-inch hole and 80 pounds of concrete per post, with extra depth and width where the sand is loose or wet and the most at gates and corners. Every wood fence also gets a rot board unless you'd rather skip it.
