<div class="callout76">
<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Start with your dog's habits, then fit the material to your lot and your deed restrictions. A 6-foot board-on-board or solid vinyl fence handles jumpers, squeezers, and fence-line barkers in most backyards. Diggers need something below grade, and on acreage, no-climb wire covers a lot of ground for far less than privacy fencing.</p>
</div>
<h2>Match the fence to how your dog gets out</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Diggers.</strong> The most common soil in Montgomery County is Conroe loamy fine sand, and the top layer digs easily. Heavy rain can also wash small gaps under an otherwise sound fence. Buried wire, an L-shaped wire footer, or a concrete mow strip along the base stops most of it.</li>
<li><strong>Jumpers.</strong> A dog that clears 4 feet from a standstill needs 6. Avoid horizontal rails or brackets on the dog's side that work as a ladder.</li>
<li><strong>Climbers.</strong> Chain link gives toeholds. Smooth board or vinyl faces don't.</li>
<li><strong>Squeezers.</strong> Small dogs get through gaps between pickets, under gates, and beside gate posts. Board-on-board or no-gap vinyl panels close those off.</li>
<li><strong>Fence-line barkers.</strong> Dogs that react to every passing neighbor or dog usually settle down behind a solid privacy fence, because they can't see what sets them off.</li>
</ul>
<p>Our <a href="/fence-styles/pet/">pet and dog fence page</a> covers the build options in more detail.</p>
<h2>Your deed restrictions may narrow the choice</h2>
<p>Around Magnolia, the HOA often decides the material before the dog does. A few examples from neighborhoods we work in:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><a href="/neighborhoods/ranch-crest/">Ranch Crest</a>, Sections III and IV:</strong> wood, brick, wrought iron, and vinyl are allowed; chain link, wire, and stand-alone mesh are not, and dog runs must be behind the house. A chain link dog run is off the table there, so a wood or vinyl enclosure is the usual answer.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/neighborhoods/westwood/">Westwood</a>:</strong> the 2016 guidelines allow chain link along with wood, wrought iron, and vinyl, with written ACC approval. A vinyl-coated chain link run is often the lowest-cost way to give a dog its own space.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/neighborhoods/walden-on-lake-conroe/">Walden</a>:</strong> proposed updated restrictions for Section 4 would limit waterfront fences behind the house to 4-foot black open ornamental metal, with no chain link or wire. A 4-foot open fence won't hold a jumper, so a smaller enclosed dog area near the house may be the practical fix. Check whether those rules have been adopted for your section.</li>
</ul>
<p>Rules change and vary by section, so we confirm the current version for your lot before we draw anything.</p>
<h2>On acreage</h2>
<p>Fencing a few acres in privacy wood gets expensive fast. Most acreage owners around Magnolia and Pinehurst fence a smaller dog yard near the house in wood, vinyl, or <a href="/materials/chain-link/">chain link</a>, then enclose the larger area with woven no-climb wire. The tight mesh keeps paws and heads from going through, and it's the same wire many owners already use for horses. See <a href="/materials/farm-ranch/">farm and ranch materials</a>. We also build electric fence, on its own or alongside wire, and it's worth asking whether it suits your dogs and your property.</p>
<p>Plan for low spots along a creek or drainage where the ground washes out under the wire, and for neighbors' animals. Horses are part of life in places like <a href="/neighborhoods/indigo-lake-estates/">Indigo Lake Estates</a> and the non-golf section of <a href="/neighborhoods/lake-windcrest/">Lake Windcrest</a>, and <a href="/neighborhoods/cimarron-country/">Cimarron Country's</a> Section 1 restrictions allow up to one horse or cow per acre. That matters for a dog that roams. Under <a href="https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.822.htm#822.013" rel="noopener">Health and Safety Code Section 822.013</a>, a person who sees a dog attacking livestock, or the livestock's owner, may kill the dog without liability to its owner, and the dog's owner is liable for the damage. A separate, secure dog yard protects both sides of the line.</p>
<h2>On lake and golf lots</h2>
<p>Lots in <a href="/neighborhoods/bentwater/">Bentwater</a>, <a href="/neighborhoods/april-sound/">April Sound</a>, and other west Lake Conroe communities often limit the lake or fairway side to open ornamental fencing. Standard ornamental picket spacing will hold a big dog but can let a small one slip through. Some aluminum styles come with tighter-spaced pickets along the bottom, and a mesh liner is another option, but check with your architectural committee first, since both change the look.</p>
<h2>What it costs, roughly</h2>
<p>Our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a> lists installed ranges per foot: chain link $18&ndash;$40, pressure-treated pine $25&ndash;$35, cedar $30&ndash;$47, vinyl $25&ndash;$70, and aluminum or steel $40&ndash;$100. No-climb and other ranch wire is priced at a site visit because acreage and terrain vary so much. A dig barrier or mow strip adds to the base price, and tear-out of an old fence is quoted separately where it applies.</p>
<h2>Gates are the weak point</h2>
<p>A fence is only as secure as its gates, and gates get left open by guests, kids, and service crews. For dog yards we recommend self-closing hinges and a self-latching latch, a gap at the bottom no bigger than the rest of the fence, and a lockable latch on any gate a lawn crew uses. Gate posts get the deepest footings on the job, so the gate doesn't sag and open a gap at the latch. If an older gate has already started to drop, our guide to <a href="/resources/fix-sagging-fence-gate/">fixing a sagging gate</a> can help.</p>
