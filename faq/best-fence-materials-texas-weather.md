<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> No single material wins on every count here. Ornamental aluminum handles damp air and lakeside exposure best, open metal and chain link handle wind best, and cedar on well-set posts is still the best value for full privacy. The ground under a fence decides its life as much as the material on top.</p>
</div>
<h2>Four things our weather throws at a fence</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sun and heat.</strong> Summer highs in the mid-90s fade stain, dry out wood, and make vinyl expand and contract through the day.</li>
<li><strong>Long wet spells.</strong> Roughly 51 inches of rain a year, often arriving in bunches, keeps the bottom of a fence damp for days at a time.</li>
<li><strong>Storm wind and falling limbs.</strong> Tropical systems and spring storms push on solid panels, and on wooded lots a dropped pine limb does more damage than the wind itself.</li>
<li><strong>Sand over clay.</strong> Most of Montgomery County has sandy loam on top and a slow-draining clay subsoil below. Sand gives a post little side grip, and water can sit at the clay layer inside a post hole.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Matching the material to the property</h2>
<p>On acreage outside Magnolia and Pinehurst, the usual mix is wire across the pasture, rail along the road, and wood or vinyl privacy only near the house. Wire fences live or die by their corners, so put the money into H-braces before a heavier wire; our <a href="/materials/farm-ranch/">farm and ranch materials</a> page covers the options.</p>
<p>Along the west shore of Lake Conroe, in Bentwater, April Sound, Walden, and their neighbors, damp air off the water and sprinkler overspray favor aluminum, and the view rules usually do too. On subdivision lots where privacy is the point, a well-built cedar fence remains the most common answer. Our <a href="/faq/best-fence-for-wind/">wind page</a> covers which styles shed a gust and which ones catch it.</p>
<h2>What no material can make up for</h2>
<p>Most of the fence failures we're called to fix start at the posts, not the pickets or panels. We set every post in concrete, a minimum of 24 inches down with 80 lb in each hole, adding depth and width wherever we hit loose sand or a hole that fills with water. Posts that hang a gate or turn a corner go deeper still. Our <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">post-setting guide for local soil</a> explains how we decide hole by hole.</p>
