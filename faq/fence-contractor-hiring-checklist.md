<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Get every bid in writing, then compare what's actually being built: material quality and installation standards like post depth, concrete, and how rails attach. After that, compare the paperwork behind it: insurance certificate, warranty terms, who handles 811 and approvals, and when money changes hands.</p>
</div>
<h2>What you're getting</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Material grade.</strong> Cedar or pressure-treated pine, picket thickness, metal gauge or wall thickness, wire type for pasture fence. This is where a cheaper bid usually saves money.</li>
<li><strong>Footage and height</strong>, measured on site rather than from an aerial photo, especially on wooded lots in places like Cimarron Country or Clear Creek Forest, where the line bends around trees.</li>
<li><strong>Gates</strong> described, with width, framing, hinges, and latches. A 12- or 16-foot drive gate on acreage is a different item from a walk gate.</li>
<li><strong>Tear-out and haul-off</strong> of the old fence. On our quotes it's listed where it applies. It isn't part of the standard per-foot price of a new fence, so check whether each bid includes it or leaves it out.</li>
<li><strong>Cleanup</strong>, including what happens to the sand and clay from the post holes.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How it's built</h2>
<p>This is where bids differ most and say the least. Ask each contractor to put these in writing:</p>
<ul>
<li>Post depth and the amount of concrete per post, plus what changes at gate and corner posts. Much of the ground around Magnolia is sand over slow-draining clay, so a post that's fine in one yard can lean in the next. Ours start at 24 inches with 80 lb of concrete and go deeper for taller fences, for gate and corner posts depending on gate size, and in loose or wet soil. Our <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">Montgomery County soil guide</a> explains why.</li>
<li>How rails attach to posts. We use Simpson brackets instead of toe-nailing.</li>
<li>How many rails. Every wood fence we build has three.</li>
<li>Fasteners. We use hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails, which hold better and resist rust streaks.</li>
<li>Whether a rot board is included. Ours is 6 inches standard.</li>
<li>On acreage, how corners and braces are built, since they hold the tension on a wire fence.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Who approves your fence, and who files</h2>
<p>Around here the approval step depends on where the lot sits, and a good bid should say who handles it. Inside the City of Magnolia, a fence needs a city permit; the city's current schedule lists a $65 permit fee plus a $50 administrative fee. The City of Montgomery also lists fences among projects that need a permit. Most of Pinehurst and the acreage around Magnolia is unincorporated Montgomery County, where a typical fence usually needs no county building permit unless the lot is in a mapped floodplain. Our <a href="/faq/fence-permits-magnolia-montgomery/">permit FAQ</a> sorts out which applies.</p>
<p>We're glad to apply for the permit where the city allows a contractor to. Sometimes the homeowner has to submit it. If we apply and pay the fee, it's passed through on your invoice. Either way we help prepare the paperwork and the drawing.</p>
<p>HOA or POA approval is separate, and it often sets the schedule. <a href="/neighborhoods/walden-on-lake-conroe/">Walden's</a> Architectural Control Committee, for example, meets every other Tuesday, and <a href="/neighborhoods/ranch-crest/">Ranch Crest's</a> newer sections require ACC approval before any fence goes up. A contractor who offers to start before approval is in writing is putting you at risk, not them. Our <a href="/resources/hoa-fence-approval-magnolia-tx/">HOA approval guide</a> covers the packet.</p>
<h2>Paperwork and protection</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Insurance certificate</strong> showing general liability, and whether the company carries workers' comp. See <a href="/faq/licensed-and-insured/">licensed and insured</a> for why Texas makes this the thing to check.</li>
<li><strong>811 locates</strong>: who places the request for a new installation, and whether they'll give you the ticket number. Details are in our <a href="/faq/texas-811/">Texas 811 FAQ</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Warranty in writing</strong>, with its length, start date, exclusions, and which work it covers. Ours is an 18-month limited workmanship warranty on runs we fully build; see the <a href="/warranty/">warranty page</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Payment schedule</strong>: what's due at signing, what's due at completion, and whether the balance waits until after the walkthrough.</li>
<li><strong>Financing</strong>, if you need it, and whose program it is. Ours is through Hearth; see <a href="/financing/">financing</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Extra checks for lake and acreage properties</h2>
<p>On a Bentwater, Walden, or Grand Harbor lot, ask how the contractor will handle the SJRA's 201-foot line and the architectural committee's rules for the water side. On five acres off FM 1774, ask whether they'll walk the full line with you, how they'll handle brush and creek crossings, and whether the gate posts are sized for a future opener.</p>
<p>Last, look at work they've finished nearby, in person if you can drive past it. A fence that's three or four years old shows more than a photo of one built last week. When you're ready to compare, <a href="/contact/">book a free estimate</a> and hold our written quote up against this list. Current per-foot ranges by material are in the <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>.</p>
