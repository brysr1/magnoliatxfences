<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> A wood fence here often lasts 10 to 20 years, with cedar that's stained on schedule at the long end and bare pine at the short end. Vinyl, aluminum, steel, and composite often reach 20 years or more. Post setting and upkeep shift those numbers more than any brand name.</p>

<h2>Typical ranges we see</h2>
<p>These are general ranges for fences built and maintained reasonably well in our area, not promises. A fence on a shady, wet lot or one that takes a falling limb can fall short of them, and a well-kept one can beat them.</p>

<h2>Where fences wear out first on local lots</h2>
<p>The first few inches above the ground take the worst of it. Most yards around Magnolia sit on sandy loam over a slower-draining clay layer, the pattern the USDA describes for the <a href="https://soilseries.sc.egov.usda.gov/OSD_Docs/C/CONROE.html" rel="noopener">Conroe soil series</a>, whose typical profile was taken near Willis in Montgomery County. The sand drains fast at the surface, but water stops on the clay, so the base of a wood post can sit damp for days after a heavy rain. Add pine needles banked against the bottom rail and a sprinkler head that hits the same boards every morning, and that strip is where rot begins. Our <a href="/faq/why-fence-posts-fail/">post failure FAQ</a> goes deeper on the soil.</p>
<p>Termites work the same strip. Native subterranean termites live in soil across Texas, and wooded lots with old pine stumps give them plenty to eat. Texas A&amp;M's <a href="https://urbanentomology.tamu.edu/urban-pests/termites/formosan/" rel="noopener">Formosan termite page</a> lists detections in neighboring Harris County but not in Montgomery County, so the termites in most Magnolia fences are the slower-working native kind. Our <a href="/resources/termite-damage-wood-fence/">termite damage guide</a> shows what to look for.</p>
<p>Gates come next. A drive gate on an acreage tract or a wide double gate on a subdivision lot puts constant leverage on its hinge post, and a post that has loosened slightly shows up as a gate that drags. On lake lots in Bentwater, Walden, or April Sound, steel hardware near the water rusts sooner than the same parts inland.</p>
<h2>Storms end more fences than age</h2>
<p>On wooded lots, a lot of fences never reach the end of their range. When Hurricane Beryl came through in July 2024, the National Weather Service's <a href="https://www.weather.gov/hgx/beryl2024" rel="noopener">storm summary</a> listed a 69 mph gust at the Conroe ASOS station. Wind like that lays down privacy panels and drops pines across pasture lines. Storm damage, falling limbs, and flooding are outside our workmanship warranty, and insurance usually pays depreciated value on an older fence, so the age of the fence matters when you decide between patching and replacing. Our <a href="/resources/fence-storm-damage-insurance-claim/">storm damage claim guide</a> covers the paperwork.</p>
<h2>Habits that add years</h2>
<ul>
<li>Stain or seal wood within the first few months, then every 2 to 4 years. Runs with full south or west sun need it sooner.</li>
<li>Turn sprinkler heads so they water the lawn, not the fence.</li>
<li>Rake pine straw and leaves away from the bottom rail each fall.</li>
<li>Replace a rotted rot board before the damage climbs into the pickets.</li>
<li>Trim limbs that hang over the line before hurricane season starts June 1.</li>
<li>Fix a dragging gate early, before it bends the frame or loosens the post further.</li>
<li>Walk acreage lines after storms and clear limbs off the wire.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What we build in from day one</h2>
<p>Most of a wood fence's life is decided when it goes in. Ours get three rails, a 6-inch rot board, Simpson brackets at the rail-to-post joints, hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails, and posts at least 24 inches deep in 80 pounds of concrete, deeper for taller fences, gates, and corners. Galvanized steel posts behind wood pickets are an option where ground-line rot is the main worry.</p>
<h2>When age stops being worth fighting</h2>
<p>A fence with a handful of bad boards and solid posts is worth repairing. Once posts are leaning or soft along long stretches, the fence has reached the end of its life no matter how good the pickets look. Our <a href="/maintenance-repair/">repair team</a> can tell you which side of that line your fence is on, and a free estimate puts both prices in writing.</p>
