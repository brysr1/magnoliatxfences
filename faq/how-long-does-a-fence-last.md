<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> A wood fence here often lasts 10 to 20 years, with cedar that's stained on schedule at the long end and bare pine at the short end. Vinyl, aluminum, steel, and composite often reach 20 years or more. Post setting and upkeep shift those numbers more than any brand name.</p>
</div>
<h2>Typical ranges we see</h2>
<p>These are general ranges for fences built and maintained reasonably well in our area, not promises. A fence on a shady, wet lot or one that takes a falling limb can fall short of them, and a well-kept one can beat them.</p>

<h2>Where fences wear out first on local lots</h2>
<p>The first few inches above the ground take the worst of it. Sandy topsoil drains fast at the surface, but the clay layer beneath holds water, so the base of a wood post can sit damp for days after a heavy rain. Add pine needles banked against the bottom rail and a sprinkler head that hits the same boards every morning, and that strip is where rot begins.</p>
<p>Gates come next. A drive gate on an acreage tract or a wide double gate on a subdivision lot puts constant leverage on its hinge post, and a post that has loosened slightly shows up as a gate that drags. On lake lots, steel hardware near the water rusts sooner than the same parts inland.</p>
<h2>Habits that add years</h2>
<ul>
<li>Stain or seal wood within the first few months, then every 2 to 4 years.</li>
<li>Turn sprinkler heads so they water the lawn, not the fence.</li>
<li>Rake needles and leaves away from the bottom rail each fall.</li>
<li>Replace a rotted rot board before the damage climbs into the pickets.</li>
<li>Fix a dragging gate early, before it bends the frame or loosens the post further.</li>
<li>Walk acreage lines after storms and clear limbs off the wire.</li>
</ul>

<h2>When age stops being worth fighting</h2>
<p>A fence with a handful of bad boards and solid posts is worth repairing. Once posts are leaning or soft along long stretches, the fence has reached the end of its life no matter how good the pickets look.
