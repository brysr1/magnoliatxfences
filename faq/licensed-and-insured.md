<div class="callout76">
<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Insured, yes. Licensed is a different matter: Texas doesn't issue a state license for fence contractors, or for general contractors. Ask for a certificate of insurance instead, and if the job includes a powered gate, ask who is doing the electrical work.</p>
</div>
<h2>Why a license number won't settle it</h2>
<p>Texas once had home builder registration through the Texas Residential Construction Commission, but that agency was abolished on September 1, 2009. Nothing replaced it for fencing. A company calling itself licensed may have a local registration, a sales tax permit, or simply nothing, and none of those say much about how the fence will be built.</p>
<p>What a permit office checks is the project, not the builder. Inside the City of Magnolia, the fence permit covers footage, material, and height. In unincorporated Montgomery County, where most acreage around Magnolia sits, there's usually no permit at all, so the homeowner is the only one vetting the contractor.</p>
<h2>The two policies that matter</h2>
<p><strong>General liability</strong> pays when the contractor damages something that isn't the fence: a broken irrigation line, a cracked driveway from a loaded truck, a neighbor's car, a dock rail on a lake lot.</p>
<p><strong>Workers' compensation</strong> covers the crew if someone gets hurt on your property. In Texas, workers' comp is optional for most private employers, and companies that opt out are called non-subscribers. That's legal, but you'd want to know it before an auger is running in your backyard.</p>
<p>When you ask for proof, have the certificate sent from the contractor's insurance agent rather than accepting a photo. Check that the company name matches the quote and that the policy dates cover your install. We carry both general liability and workers' compensation, and we send certificates on request.</p>
<h2>Gate openers bring in a real license</h2>
<p>Electrical work is one area where Texas does license the trade. The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation requires non-exempt electrical work to be done by licensed electricians working through a licensed electrical contractor. Running 120- or 240-volt power out to a drive gate on a Magnolia acreage tract is generally that kind of work. Low-voltage control wiring, like a keypad or safety sensor circuit, may fall under an exemption, and cities can set their own rules.</p>
<p>A solar operator often sidesteps the question, which is part of why it's popular on long driveways. If you want a wired opener, ask who will run the power feed and confirm they're licensed. Our <a href="/fence-styles/gates/">gates and openers page</a> covers both types.</p>
