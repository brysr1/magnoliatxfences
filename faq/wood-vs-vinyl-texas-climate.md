<p><strong>Short answer:</strong> Neither material breaks down in our climate when it's built well, so the better pick depends on the lot and the owner. Cedar suits people who don't mind a refinish every few years and live where wood is the expected look. Vinyl suits people who would rather rinse a fence than stain it, once they've confirmed their HOA allows it.</p>
</div>
<h2>What our weather does to each material</h2>
<p>Long-term averages at Conroe, the closest major weather station to most of our jobs, come to roughly 51 inches of rain a year, with July and August highs in the mid-90s. That swing between soaking weeks and baking weeks is the real test. Wood absorbs water when it's wet, dries hard in August, and repeats the cycle until boards check, cup, and turn gray. Vinyl doesn't absorb water, so rain mostly matters to it as a source of mildew.</p>
<p>The ground matters too. Much of the soil around Magnolia is sand over slow-draining clay, so after a heavy rain the bottom of a fence line can stay wet for days. That's hard on the bottom of a wood fence and doesn't bother vinyl, which is why every wood fence we build gets a rot board. Our <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">soil guide</a> explains what's under a typical yard here.</p>
<p>Shade shifts the picture. Plenty of lots around Magnolia and Pinehurst sit under tall pines and hardwoods; <a href="/neighborhoods/cimarron-country/">Cimarron Country</a>, <a href="/neighborhoods/carriage-hills/">Carriage Hills</a>, and <a href="/neighborhoods/clear-creek-forest/">Clear Creek Forest</a> are known for wooded lots. Under that canopy, wood stays damp longer and needles pile up along the bottom rail, while vinyl picks up a green film on its north face. Neither is serious, but the wood problem costs boards if it's ignored and the vinyl problem costs a bucket of soapy water. Wooded lots also mean falling limbs, which matters in the repair section below.</p>
<p>Full sun is the opposite case. A west-facing run on an open acreage tract fades stain faster than a shaded one, and vinyl panels in direct afternoon sun expand. Good vinyl is made for that movement as long as the installer leaves room for it; our page on <a href="/faq/will-vinyl-warp-in-heat/">whether vinyl warps in heat</a> covers the details.</p>
<h2>Where each one fits on local lots</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Deed-restricted subdivisions:</strong> many architectural guidelines spell out a wood privacy style and a stain color, while some allow vinyl. <a href="/neighborhoods/ranch-crest/">Ranch Crest's</a> newer sections, for example, allow wood, brick, wrought iron, and vinyl. Read your rules before pricing either one. Our <a href="/resources/hoa-fence-approval-magnolia-tx/">HOA approval guide</a> walks through the paperwork.</li>
<li><strong>Acreage around Magnolia and Pinehurst:</strong> vinyl rail along the road frontage saves painting a long stretch of wood, while a cedar privacy section around the backyard keeps the cost of the whole project down.</li>
<li><strong>West Lake Conroe waterfront and fairway lots:</strong> solid privacy in either material is often limited where the view matters, so the answer there may be <a href="/materials/ornamental-steel-aluminum/">ornamental aluminum or steel</a> with a privacy section only between neighbors. Both run $40&ndash;$100 a foot; aluminum won't rust, though it can pit over time.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Upkeep, year by year</h2>
<p>Every cedar or pine fence we build gets three rails, a 6-inch rot board, Simpson brackets at the rails, and hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails, which takes care of most of what humidity does to the structure. The finish is the owner's part: a penetrating stain within the first few months, then a fresh coat roughly every 2 to 4 years, sooner on runs that take full sun. Our <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining and power washing</a> crew can handle that for you. Termites are another wood-only concern here; see our <a href="/resources/termite-damage-wood-fence/">termite damage guide</a>.</p>
<p>Vinyl's routine is a hose and mild soap once or twice a year. The trade-off shows up after an impact. A mower or falling limb that would cost one picket on a wood fence usually means a whole vinyl panel, and matching the color on an older vinyl fence isn't always simple. On a heavily wooded lot, that's worth weighing.</p>
<h2>How the cost compares over time</h2>
<p>Our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a> lists pressure-treated pine at $25&ndash;$35 a foot installed, cedar at $30&ndash;$47, and vinyl at $25&ndash;$70, with vinyl privacy panels starting around $50. So a vinyl privacy fence generally costs more to install than a cedar or pine privacy fence of the same height, while vinyl 3-rail on acreage frontage can land close to wood. Within vinyl, thicker, higher-quality material costs more but resists sagging and cracking in the heat.</p>
<p>The gap narrows over the years because wood carries refinishing costs that vinyl doesn't. If you expect to stay in the house a long time, add a few rounds of staining to the wood price before deciding which fence is cheaper. If the up-front number is the hurdle, we offer <a href="/financing/">financing through Hearth</a>.</p>
