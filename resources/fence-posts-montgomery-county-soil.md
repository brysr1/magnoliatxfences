<p>Most of the ground we dig around Magnolia, Pinehurst, and Montgomery looks easy for the first couple of feet. The auger runs through tan sand quickly, and then things change: the hole gets tighter, the color turns mottled red and gray, and on some lots the bottom starts filling with water before we're done. How a post is set in that kind of ground decides whether the fence stays straight.</p>
<p>This page covers how we set posts on a new fence. If you already have a post that's leaning, our guide to <a href="/resources/fix-leaning-fence-post-sandy-soil/">fixing a leaning post in sandy soil</a> is the better read.</p>

<h2>What's under a typical Magnolia-area yard</h2>
<p>The most common soil in Montgomery County, according to the NRCS soil survey, is Conroe loamy fine sand, which covers about 11 percent of the county. The runners-up are Fetzer loamy fine sand and two fine sandy loams, Splendora and Woodville. The USDA describes the Conroe series as loamy fine sand on top with sandy clay loam and clay underneath that carries plinthite (the reddish, iron-rich layer most people call ironstone), and it drains slowly.</p>
<p>That's a different problem from the heavy clay closer to Houston. Conroe-series clay is kaolinitic, a kind that doesn't swell and shrink much, though some parts of the county do have heavier clays. On most lots we work, the issue isn't ground heaving. It's that sand doesn't grip a post well, and the layer under it holds water.</p>

<h2>Three things we watch for in the hole</h2>
<h3>Sand that won't hold its shape</h3>
<p>Dry sand caves in from the sides as you dig, so a hole can end up wide at the top and narrow at the bottom. That cone shape puts the least concrete where the post needs the most support. In deep sand we dig deeper and wider so the footing has more to bear against.</p>
<h3>Ironstone and clay at depth</h3>
<p>The plinthite layer is hard enough to stop an auger. Quitting there leaves a post shallower than it should be, so we work through it to reach full depth rather than settling for wherever the digging got hard.</p>
<h3>Water standing in the hole</h3>
<p>Because the subsoil drains slowly, rain can perch on top of the clay, and a fresh hole on a low lot may partly fill within minutes. Pour concrete into that and the water thins the mix, leaving a soft footing. The water comes out before the concrete goes in, and on a soaked day it's better to reschedule a section than to set posts in mud.</p>

<h2>Our baseline, and when we go past it</h2>
<p>Concrete goes around every post we set. On a wood fence, each post gets a hole at least 24 inches deep and 80 pounds of concrete, and we treat that as the floor, not the goal. Loose sand, wet ground, and taller fences all call for more.</p>

<h2>Gates and corners get their own spec</h2>
<p>A gate post works like a lever. A wide gate hanging from one post pulls sideways at the top all day, and every slam jolts the footing. In sand, that's where a lean shows up first, so gate posts get the most depth and concrete we put in the ground. Drive gates get posts sized to the gate's weight and width. Corner and end posts come next, especially on chain link, where they hold the tension of the whole run.</p>
<p>Wire fences on acreage work differently. Long runs of field wire or no-climb rely on braced corner assemblies, such as H-braces, to hold tension, not on concrete alone. See <a href="/acreage-ranch-fencing/">acreage and ranch fencing</a> for how we lay those out. Our short page on <a href="/faq/why-fence-posts-fail/">why fence posts fail</a> lists what goes wrong when any of this is skipped.</p>

<h2>Lake lots, creek bottoms, and fill</h2>
<p>Lots on the west side of Lake Conroe and along the Spring Creek and Lake Creek bottoms bring wetter ground, and holes there can hit saturated sand quickly. On lakefront lots we also confirm where the property line sits relative to the SJRA's 201-foot contour before posts go in near the water. Newer lots built up with fill get a closer look too, since fill is often looser than the native ground beside it.</p>

<h2>Protecting the post at the ground line</h2>
<p>The ground line is where wood posts rot. We put a rot board on every wood fence unless you ask us not to, which keeps the pickets off the soil, and we recommend keeping mulch and soil pulled back from the posts. If you want a wood fence whose posts outlast the boards, ask us about galvanized steel posts behind the wood, since steel won't rot or feed termites at grade. Vinyl and ornamental metal posts still need the same depth and concrete; the soil doesn't care what the fence is made of.</p>
