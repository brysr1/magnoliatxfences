<p>When people compare steel vs. wood fence posts, they're usually planning a wood privacy fence and wondering what should hold it up. From the street the fence looks about the same either way. Underneath, the two posts age very differently in the sandy, damp ground around Magnolia and Pinehurst.</p>
<p>Here's how galvanized steel and pressure-treated pine posts compare on the things that matter here, and how we'd choose between them on your lot.</p>

<h2>Cost up front</h2>
<p>Treated pine posts are the standard on most wood fences and the budget choice. Galvanized steel posts usually add to the price, both for the material and for the brackets that connect wood rails to a round or channel-shaped post. How much depends on the length of the fence, its height, and how many gates and corners it has. Your written quote will show both options if you ask, and our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a> explains what goes into a typical wood fence price.</p>

<h2>Rot at the ground line</h2>
<p>This is the big one. A wood post fails where it meets the soil. Most lots in this part of Montgomery County have sandy loam on top of a slower-draining clay layer, so after a long rain the base of a post can sit wet for days. Pine needles and mulch piled against the fence keep it damp even longer.</p>
<p>Ground-contact treated pine holds up to that for years, but it's still wood, and a post that's soft at grade is where an older fence breaks. Galvanized steel doesn't rot. The weak spot on steel is rust where the zinc coating gets scratched or where water sits against the post, which is why a footing that sheds water away from the post matters on either kind.</p>

<h2>Termites</h2>
<p>Native subterranean termites live in the soil across the area, and wooded lots with old pine stumps give them plenty to eat. A wood post in the ground is an easy entry point. Steel posts give them nothing to eat at grade, though the rails, pickets, and rot board are still wood. Our article on <a href="/resources/termite-damage-wood-fence/">termite damage on a wood fence</a> shows where they usually start.</p>

<h2>Strength in sandy soil</h2>
<p>A steel post is stiffer than a wood post of similar size and won't warp or twist as it dries. That helps on tall fences and long runs. What it won't do is fix a weak footing. Loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways, and a steel post in a shallow hole leans just like a wood one, starting at the gates and corners.</p>
<p>So the setting rules stay the same no matter what the post is made of. Concrete goes around every post. On a wood fence that means at least 24 inches deep with 80 pounds of concrete as the floor, deeper or wider in loose sand or wet ground, and more at gates and corners, with water pumped or bailed out of the hole before the pour. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting posts in Montgomery County soil</a> walks through it.</p>

<h2>Looks and HOA rules</h2>
<p>From the finished side, a wood fence on steel posts looks like any other wood fence, since the boards cover the posts. From the back you'll see gray metal posts instead of wood. Some owners like that clean look, and some prefer to cover the posts with wood trim.</p>
<p>Many HOAs around Magnolia regulate fence height, material, and which side faces out. Fewer say anything about post material, but it's worth checking your architectural guidelines before you decide, especially if the post side faces a street or common area.</p>

<h2>Lifespan and future repairs</h2>
<p>A wood fence here often lasts about 10 to 20 years, and it's usually the posts that end it. With steel posts, the frame can outlast the first set of pickets. When the boards wear out, you can often re-picket on the same posts instead of pulling footings and starting over.</p>
<p>Wood posts are easier to patch one at a time. A single rotted or leaning wood post can be cut out and replaced without special parts.</p>

<h2>Which one we'd pick</h2>
<p>If you plan to stay in the house a long time, the lot stays damp after rain, or termites have already been a problem, steel posts are usually worth the extra cost. If you're watching the budget or the fence is a back property line you may change in a few years, well-set treated pine posts are a sound choice.</p>
