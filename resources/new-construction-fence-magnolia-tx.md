<p>Montgomery County has grown fast. It had 620,443 people at the 2020 Census, and by July 2025 the Census Bureau put the figure at 781,194, roughly a quarter more in five years. Around Magnolia, that growth shows up as new sections in communities like Audubon, new subdivisions along FM 1488 and FM 1774, and custom homes going up on acreage tracts outside town.</p>
<p>Fencing a brand-new home is a little different from replacing an old fence. The lot lines are fresh, the ground was just graded, irrigation and landscaping may not be in yet, and there's often a builder fence or a builder schedule to work around.</p>

<h2>Builder fence, upgrade, or start from scratch?</h2>
<p>In many new subdivisions, the builder puts up a basic fence, often pine, along the back and sides. You can keep it, ask whether the builder offers an upgrade, or have your own fence company build the yard you want. Builder fences are built to a price, and in newer Magnolia neighborhoods they tend to need repairs or replacement sooner than owners expect.</p>
<p>If you're replacing or extending a builder fence, the HOA usually expects the same height, style, and color as the rest of the street, so even a cedar upgrade may need to match the original look. New sections often have the most detailed guidelines, and the association may still be run by the developer. Get the fence application in early; our <a href="/resources/hoa-fence-approval-magnolia-tx/">HOA approval guide</a> walks through it. If the builder fence is still under the builder's warranty, ask before anyone changes it.</p>

<h2>Find the pins before you pick a line</h2>
<p>Look in your closing papers for the survey and the recorded plat. The plat shows lot lines, easements, and building lines. The survey shows where the corner pins were set. On a new lot, pins can get buried under fill or knocked loose by equipment, so walk the lot with the survey in hand before the fence goes in. If you can't find them, a surveyor can re-stake the corners. See our <a href="/faq/property-survey/">property survey</a> page.</p>
<p>On acreage, getting the line right matters even more. An angle that's off by just one degree over a 600-foot run puts the far corner about ten feet from where it should be.</p>

<h2>Easements, swales, and detention ponds</h2>
<p>New subdivisions are engineered to move water: shallow swales along rear lot lines, drainage easements between homes, and detention ponds at the edge of a section. A solid fence across a swale can back water up into your yard or your neighbor's, and whoever holds the easement may be able to make you remove anything that blocks it. Check the plat for drainage and utility easements along your back and side lines, and check your guidelines for rules on lots next to ponds and greenbelts, which often call for open fencing. Our <a href="/faq/flood-zone-easement-fencing/">flood zone and easement</a> page goes further.</p>

<h2>Septic, irrigation, and lines nobody has mapped</h2>
<p>Texas 811 marks public utility lines, and we request locates on every job. On a new lot, those lines went in recently, and anything private that was added after the house won't be marked at all. That includes irrigation, landscape lighting, gas or electric to an outdoor kitchen or pool equipment, and lines to a detached shop.</p>
<p>Many homes on the acreage around Magnolia use septic systems, and some are aerobic systems with spray heads in the yard. Tanks, lines, and spray areas aren't marked by 811, so flag them yourself or have the installer's drawing ready for the estimate. Leave a path so the service truck can still reach the tank once the fence is up. More on what gets marked is on our <a href="/faq/texas-811/">Texas 811</a> page.</p>

<h2>Time it with grading and landscaping</h2>
<p>Fill and regraded soil settle, especially through the first few hard rains. Posts set in soft backfill near the slab or along a freshly cut swale are the ones that lean in year one. When we dig on a new lot, we look at how firm the ground is and go deeper where it's loose.</p>
<p>Order matters too. A sequence that works well on most new builds is final grade first, then the fence, then irrigation and sod. That way nobody digs through new sprinkler lines and the sod isn't torn up by equipment. If your landscaper is already booked, coordinate dates so one crew isn't undoing the other's work.</p>

<h2>On acreage, plan the gate before the driveway is poured</h2>
<p>If you're building on acreage and want an automatic entry gate someday, decide where it goes before the driveway is poured. A sleeve or conduit under the drive for operator wiring is cheap during construction and saves cutting concrete later. The same goes for power: if you'd rather have a wired operator than solar, the electrician can plan the feed along with the house. Set the gate back far enough from the road that a trailer isn't sticking into traffic while it swings. Our <a href="/resources/automatic-gate-opener-acreage/">gate opener guide</a> covers the choices.</p>
