<p>Most of the wood fences we build around Magnolia, Pinehurst, and Montgomery are either cedar or pressure-treated pine, and the cedar vs. pine fence question comes up on almost every estimate. Both make a good privacy fence. They just wear differently in our heat and humidity, and the cheaper board up front isn't always the cheaper fence ten years later.</p>
<p>Here's how the two compare on the things that decide it for most owners: price, rot and bugs, how the boards hold their shape, upkeep, and lifespan.</p>

<h2>Cedar vs. pine fence cost</h2>
<p>Pine is the budget end of wood fencing. Using the ballpark ranges in our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>, pressure-treated pine runs about $25&ndash;$35 per installed foot and cedar about $30&ndash;$47. Height, gates, tear-out of an old fence, and board-on-board versus side-by-side all move the final number.</p>
<p>On a typical backyard, that gap is real but not huge. On a long run across an acreage tract, a few dollars a foot adds up fast, which is one reason pine shows up more often on big lots outside town.</p>

<h2>Rot, termites, and the ground line</h2>
<p>The two woods protect themselves in different ways. Pine resists rot because it's pressure-treated with a preservative. Cedar's resistance is natural, from oils in the heartwood. That natural protection is strongest in the darker heartwood and weaker in the pale sapwood, so board quality matters.</p>
<p>The weak spot on either fence is the first few inches above the soil. Sandy topsoil here drains fast, but the clay layer underneath can hold water, so the base of a fence can stay damp for days after a heavy rain. Pine needles banked against the bottom rail make it worse. That's why every wood fence we build gets a rot board, and why plenty of cedar fences go up on treated pine or galvanized steel posts. Treated lumber is labeled for above-ground or ground-contact use, and posts need the ground-contact kind.</p>
<p>Cedar also holds off termites better than untreated wood. Neither species makes a fence termite-proof, so keep soil and mulch off the boards either way.</p>

<h2>Warping, twisting, and looks</h2>
<p>This is where cedar earns most of its price. Pine tends to twist, cup, and check more as it dries out through a Texas summer, and treated pine often arrives wet from the treating process, so it shrinks once it's up. Expect some gaps between pine pickets after the first hot stretch. Cedar is lighter, more stable, and stays flatter.</p>
<p>On looks, cedar has the warm, reddish tone most people picture, and it weathers to a soft silver if you leave it alone. Pine starts out pale or slightly green and looks more utilitarian bare, but it takes stain well, so a stained pine fence can look very close to cedar from the street.</p>

<h2>Staining and upkeep</h2>
<p>Both woods want the same care here. Plan on cleaning and a fresh coat of penetrating stain roughly every 2 to 4 years, sooner on runs that take full south or west sun. The difference is timing on a new fence. Cedar can usually be stained within the first few months. Wet treated pine needs to dry out first or the stain won't soak in, so ask your installer when the boards are ready. Our <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining and power washing</a> crew handles both.</p>

<p>Those lifespans assume the posts were set right. In the loose sand that covers most lots around Magnolia and Pinehurst, a shallow post leans long before the boards wear out, whatever wood is on it. We set every wood fence post at least 24 inches deep in 80 pounds of concrete, deeper and wider in loose sand or wet ground, and more at gates and corners. More on that in our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">fence posts in Montgomery County soil</a>.</p>

<h2>Which one we'd pick</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Choose pine</strong> for long runs on acreage, rental properties, a fence you plan to stain anyway, or a firm budget where every foot counts.</li>
<li><strong>Choose cedar</strong> for the backyard you look at every day, a neighborhood with a cedar spec, or if you want straighter boards and fewer gaps over time.</li>
<li><strong>Mix them</strong> when it makes sense: treated pine or steel posts with cedar pickets, or cedar near the house and pine along the back property line.</li>
</ul>
