<p>When people around Magnolia ask us for a wrought iron fence, they usually mean the look: slim black pickets, a clean top rail, maybe spear tops or a pair of arched gates at the drive. That look is everywhere here, from front yards in Magnolia subdivisions to pool areas and lake lots near Montgomery. What's changed is the metal underneath, and knowing that helps you compare quotes and pick a fence that holds up in our humidity.</p>

<h2>What "wrought iron" means today</h2>
<p>True wrought iron is a nearly pure iron that smiths heated and hammered into shape. It hasn't been made commercially in decades, and you'll mostly find it on old estates and historic buildings. The fences sold as wrought iron now are almost always one of two things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ornamental steel.</strong> Tubular steel pickets and rails, welded into panels and finished with a powder coat. It's heavy and rigid, and it's what we use where a fence takes force, like drive gates and frontage.</li>
<li><strong>Ornamental aluminum.</strong> The same styles in a lighter metal that doesn't rust. From the street, most people can't tell the two apart.</li>
</ul>
<p>Neither is a knockoff. Both give you the wrought iron look with a factory finish that needs far less work than painted iron did. Our <a href="/resources/aluminum-vs-steel-fence/">aluminum vs. steel guide</a> goes stretch by stretch through which metal fits where.</p>

<h2>How to spot a well-built wrought iron style fence</h2>
<p>Two fences can look the same in a photo and age very differently. When you compare quotes, ask about these:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The finish under the paint.</strong> On steel, ask whether the metal is galvanized or otherwise treated before it's powder-coated. That layer is what slows rust when the coating gets nicked.</li>
<li><strong>Picket and rail size.</strong> Thicker pickets and heavier rails resist bending from a dog, a falling limb, or a mower. Residential and commercial grades look similar but differ a lot in weight.</li>
<li><strong>How the panels are joined.</strong> Welded panels are stiff. Other designs use brackets or screws and can loosen over time. Neither is wrong, but you should know which one you're getting.</li>
<li><strong>Post size and spacing.</strong> Ornamental panels rely on their posts. Undersized posts are where a metal fence starts to wander out of line.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Rust and our humid weather</h2>
<p>Damp air, sprinkler overspray, and shade from the pines keep fences wet longer here than in drier parts of Texas. Steel only rusts where the coating is broken, so the habit that matters is a quick look each year. Check the bottom of the posts where a string trimmer hits them, and the gate where hardware rubs. Clean any chip, let it dry, and touch it up with matching metal paint. Aluminum skips the rust issue, which is why it's our usual pick around pools and near the water.</p>
<p>If you already have an older iron or steel fence with rust bubbling at the welds, the question is whether the rust is on the surface or has eaten through. Surface rust can be cleaned and recoated. Pickets or rails that are rusted through usually mean it's time for new panels.</p>

<h2>Wrought iron gates</h2>
<p>Gates are where this style really shows, and where it's most often built wrong. A wide metal drive gate is heavy, and all that weight hangs from one post. Steel is the usual choice for driveways, especially with an automatic opener, while aluminum works well for walk gates that match the rest of the fence. Our <a href="/fence-styles/gates/">gates page</a> covers swing and slide options and openers.</p>

<h2>Posts that keep the line straight</h2>
<p>An ornamental fence shows every lean, because the straight top rail gives it away. Most lots around Magnolia and Pinehurst have loose, sandy soil on top that doesn't grip a post sideways, so we set every post in concrete, never gravel alone, and go deeper and wider at gates and corners, with belled piers where the load is heaviest. If water seeps into a hole, it gets pumped or bailed out before the pour. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting posts in Montgomery County soil</a> explains why.</p>

Height, panel grade, picket style, decorative tops, and especially gates move a project within that range. Many owners use the wrought iron look where it's seen most, along the front of the lot or around the pool, and wood privacy along the back.</p>
