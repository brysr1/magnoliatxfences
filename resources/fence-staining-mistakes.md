<p>Most fence staining mistakes don't show up on day one. The fence looks great when the last board is done, then six months later it's blotchy, sticky in spots, or peeling along the bottom. Around Magnolia and Pinehurst, with our humidity, pine pollen, and long summer sun, the gap between a stain job that lasts three years and one that barely lasts one usually comes down to prep and timing, not the brand on the can.</p>
<p>Fall is the best window most years, with drier air and milder days. Here are the mistakes we see most when we're called out to fix a stain job, and what to do instead.</p>

<h2>The fence staining mistakes we see most</h2>

<h3>1. Staining wood that isn't dry</h3>
<p>This is the big one. Stain has to soak into the wood, and wet wood is already full. A fence that was washed yesterday, rained on two days ago, or sits in shade all day on the north side can still be holding a lot of moisture even when the surface feels dry. New pressure-treated pine is the worst case, since it often comes off the truck wet from treatment and needs time to dry out before it will take stain. Do the water-drop test in several spots, and give a washed fence a few dry days before you start.</p>

<h3>2. Skipping the wash</h3>
<p>Gray, weathered wood fibers, mildew, and the green film that grows on shady runs all sit between the stain and sound wood. Stain over them and it bonds to dirt instead of the board, and mildew can keep growing underneath. Every refinish should start with a cleaning, and a wood cleaner or brightener helps on boards that have gone silver.</p>

<h3>3. Too much pressure from the washer</h3>
<p>The opposite problem. A narrow tip held a few inches from the board will carve into soft pine, raise the grain into fuzz, and leave stripes that show right through the new stain. Use a wider fan tip, keep the wand moving with the grain, and stand back. If the boards come up fuzzy anyway, a light sanding on the worst spots helps the finish go on even.</p>

<h3>4. Picking the wrong day</h3>
<p>Even in fall, the weather can work against you. Watch for three things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Direct midday sun on the boards:</strong> stain dries too fast on hot wood, leaving lap marks where wet meets dry. Work the shady side first and follow the shade.</li>
<li><strong>Rain in the forecast:</strong> most products want a dry day or two after application. Check the label and the radar, not just today's sky.</li>
<li><strong>Cool nights and heavy dew:</strong> November mornings out here can be foggy and wet. Start after the dew burns off, stop early enough to let the finish set before evening, and stay above the minimum temperature printed on the can.</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Putting on too much</h3>
<p>More stain is not more protection. A penetrating stain should soak in, and whatever the wood can't absorb sits on top, stays tacky, collects pollen and dust, and eventually flakes. Apply a workable coat, back-brush sprayed or rolled stain into the grain, and wipe up puddles on rails and post tops before they skin over.</p>

<h3>6. Choosing a product that fights our climate</h3>
<p>Paint and thick film-forming sealers look sharp at first, then trap moisture in a fence that moves with every humid week and peel. A clear sealer with no pigment repels water but does little against sun, so the wood still grays. For most fences here, a penetrating semi-transparent or solid stain with pigment is the better bet. Our <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining and power washing</a> page explains the difference between the two.</p>

<h3>7. Staining over problems</h3>
<p>Fresh stain on a fence with leaning posts, rotted bottom boards, or loose pickets just makes a bad fence look nicer for a while. Walk the line first. If posts are moving or boards are soft at the ground, fix that before you spend money on finish, and if the fence is near the end of its life, our guide to deciding whether to <a href="/resources/repair-or-replace-fence/">repair or replace a fence</a> can help you choose. In a deed-restricted neighborhood, also confirm your color before you buy. Many HOAs around Magnolia set approved stain colors, and our <a href="/resources/hoa-fence-approval-magnolia-tx/">HOA approval guide</a> covers how to ask.</p>

<h2>A few smaller things that matter</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cover what you don't want stained:</strong> brick, concrete, plants, and the car in the driveway. Overspray travels farther than you'd think on a breezy day.</li>
<li><strong>Don't forget the bottoms and the tops:</strong> board ends and post tops soak up water fastest, so give them a full coat.</li>
<li><strong>Keep sprinklers off the fence:</strong> heads that hit the boards every morning will wash out a finish years early.</li>
</ul>
