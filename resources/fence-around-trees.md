<p>Building a fence around trees is part of almost every job we do on wooded lots in Magnolia, Pinehurst, and the west side of Lake Conroe. Loblolly pines, water oaks, and sweetgums grow right along a lot of property lines out here, and the fence has to share that ground with them for the next 15 or 20 years.</p>
<p>Done right, the trees stay healthy and the fence stays straight. Done wrong, you end up with a cracked rail where a trunk grew into it, or a post that never sets because it was jammed between roots. Here's how we plan it.</p>

<h2>Never attach the fence to a tree</h2>
<p>It's tempting to save a post by nailing a rail to a big pine. We don't, and we'd talk you out of it. A tree trunk keeps getting wider and sways in the wind, and a fence doesn't. Within a few years the tree swallows the hardware, the rail splits or pulls loose, and the nails become a wound that lets rot and insects into the trunk.</p>
<p>Instead, we stop the fence short of the trunk on each side and end each run on its own post. That small gap gives the tree room to grow and lets you pull the fence section later without cutting into wood.</p>

<h2>Routing the fence line around trunks</h2>
<p>When a tree sits right on the line, there are a few ways to handle it. Which one fits depends on the tree, the survey, and whether you need full privacy or just a boundary.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Leave a gap at the trunk:</strong> the fence stops on both sides with a small opening. Simple and cheap, but a small dog may find it.</li>
<li><strong>Jog around the tree:</strong> the fence steps in or out a few feet with short sections on each side, so the tree ends up fully inside or outside the yard with no gap.</li>
<li><strong>Move the whole line:</strong> on an acreage boundary or a back line, sometimes it makes more sense to shift a run a few feet off the trees entirely, as long as the fence stays on your side of the property line.</li>
</ul>
<p>Any jog or shift has to respect the boundary, so we work from your plat or <a href="/faq/property-survey/">property survey</a> before we pick a route. On HOA lots, check whether the jog needs to show on your approval drawing.</p>

<h2>Setting posts between roots</h2>
<p>Roots are where most tree-line problems start. The large roots near the trunk are what hold a tree upright, so cutting through them to drop a post in a perfect spot can weaken the tree and make it more likely to come down in a storm. That's a bad trade with a 70-foot pine standing over your house or your new fence.</p>
<p>Near trees we dig carefully and adjust post spacing to land between big roots instead of through them. A post might move a foot or two from where it would go on an open line, and the rails or panels get sized to match. Small feeder roots are a different story and usually aren't a concern.</p>
<p>The soil matters here too. Most lots in this zone have sandy loam on top, and loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways. We still set every post in concrete with at least a 24-inch hole and 80 lb of concrete on wood fences, and we go deeper and wider at gates and corners, belling the bottom so the post can't rock. A post squeezed between roots in loose sand needs that concrete even more. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting posts in Montgomery County soil</a> goes into the details.</p>

<h2>Limbs, needles, and storm risk</h2>
<p>Trees affect a fence from above as well as below. Before we build, look up along the line:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Overhanging limbs:</strong> dead or low branches over the fence line should come down before the fence goes up. It's easier and cheaper than fixing a crushed section later.</li>
<li><strong>Leaning or damaged trees:</strong> if a tree near the line is leaning hard or has a hollow trunk, have an arborist look at it first. A new fence won't stop a falling pine.</li>
<li><strong>Needle and leaf buildup:</strong> pine straw piles up against the bottom of a privacy fence and holds moisture. Leaving a small gap under the pickets and raking the line helps the boards last.</li>
</ul>
<p>If a tree does come down on your fence after a storm, secure the opening first if you have pets or a pool, then take photos of the tree and the damaged sections before anything gets cleaned up.</p>

<h2>Picking a fence that works with trees</h2>
<p>A wood <a href="/fence-styles/privacy/">privacy fence</a> is the easiest style to fit around trees, because each section can be cut to any width. Metal panels and vinyl come in set lengths, so tight spots between trunks sometimes need a custom-cut panel or a short section. On a wooded acreage line, field fence or split rail can bend around trees more naturally than a solid privacy fence.</p>
