<p>Most fences we build around Magnolia need someone's sign-off before the first hole is dug. In a master-planned community like Audubon or Woodforest, that's usually an architectural review committee. On a lake or golf lot in Bentwater or April Sound, it may be an architectural control committee with tighter rules for the water side. Even out on the acreage, a property owners' association in a place like High Meadow Estates can review fences.</p>
<p>The application itself isn't hard. What sends it back is a missing detail, and in many communities that means waiting for the next meeting. Here's the order we'd work in.</p>

<h2>Step 1: Find out who reviews your lot</h2>
<p>Start with your closing documents or the community's resident website. You're looking for three things: the recorded deed restrictions for your section, any fence or design guidelines the board has adopted, and who receives applications. That might be the HOA, a management company, a separate committee, or a sub-association for your section.</p>
<p>Acreage is where people get surprised. Plenty of land around Magnolia has no HOA at all, but recorded deed restrictions can still limit fence type or how close a fence can sit to the road, and some POAs review every improvement. If you're not sure what's recorded against your tract, the Montgomery County Clerk keeps the records.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Read every rule that touches a fence</h2>
<p>Pull out each line that mentions fences, walls, gates, or pools. The usual topics are:</p>
<ul>
<li>Maximum height, and whether it differs for front, side, and rear</li>
<li>Approved materials and styles, such as cedar board-on-board or ornamental metal</li>
<li>Stain or paint color, sometimes named by brand and color</li>
<li>Which side faces the street or the neighbor</li>
<li>The setback from the front of the house where side fencing has to stop</li>
<li>Rules for lots backing onto a lake, fairway, greenbelt, or pond</li>
<li>Whether a like-for-like replacement still needs approval</li>
</ul>
<p>That last one trips people up. In many communities, replacing an old fence or restaining it a different color counts as a modification that needs a new application.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Put together a complete packet</h2>
<p>Committees decide from paper, so give them everything at once. A typical packet is the application form, a site sketch, product details, and a color sample or photo. The sketch carries the most weight. It should show the lot lines, the house, the fence path and its height, every gate and its width, and the distances to the front corner of the house and to any easement. Mark where your fence ties into a neighbor's.</p>
<p>Product details mean the material, style, post and picket sizes, cap and trim, and the stain brand and color. For a metal fence, include the manufacturer's spec sheet and finish color. We help our customers put the sketch and material details together, and some boards also want the contractor's name or a copy of the quote. Send what they ask for, in the format they ask for. A few associations won't review an incomplete package at all.</p>

<h2>Lake and golf lots need a closer look</h2>
<p>On the west side of Lake Conroe, the water or fairway side of a lot is usually where the rules tighten. Open ornamental aluminum or steel at a limited height is the common approved choice facing the view, with cedar privacy allowed between homes in some communities and not others. Pool barriers often fall under a separate policy with its own hardware requirements.</p>
<p>Lakefront lots add one more party. The San Jacinto River Authority treats land up to the 201-foot contour above mean sea level as Authority Land, and its rules require a permit for encroachments there. A permanent fence below that line would likely need one, and your HOA approval doesn't cover it. The <a href="https://www.sjra.net/" rel="noopener">SJRA</a> handles those questions, and we confirm where your line and the contour fall before posts go in near the water.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Plan around the review calendar</h2>
<p>Many committees review on a set schedule, so an application that misses a cutoff waits for the next round. Plan on a few weeks from submission to written approval, and longer if you have to resubmit. If you want the fence up by a certain date, send the application before you lock in the rest of the project.</p>
<p>HOA approval is also separate from any government permit. Build inside Magnolia's city limits and you'll also need the city's fence permit, which the city says usually takes about two weeks to approve. Outside the city, unincorporated Montgomery County handles fences differently. Our <a href="/faq/fence-permits-magnolia-montgomery/">permit guide</a> sorts out which applies, and <a href="/faq/best-time-to-install-fence/">the best time to install</a> covers seasonal scheduling.</p>

<h2>If the answer is no, or "approved with conditions"</h2>
<p>Read the response closely. Most denials are fixable: a height over the limit, a stain color that isn't on the list, a fence line that runs too far toward the street, or a sketch that's missing a measurement. Change that item and resubmit. "Approved with conditions" means you can build if you make a specific change, and the finished fence should match those conditions exactly, since some communities inspect.</p>
<p>Don't start work before approval is in writing. A fence built ahead of approval can draw a violation notice and, at worst, a demand to take it down. We schedule installation once you have the approval letter, and we build what was approved.</p>
