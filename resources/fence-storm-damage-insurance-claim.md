<p>When Hurricane Beryl came through in July 2024, the National Weather Service's preliminary readings put the peak gust at the Conroe airport at 69 mph. Gusts in that range are enough to lay down a privacy fence, drop pines across a pasture line, and pull a wide gate off its hinges. A strong spring thunderstorm can do the same on a smaller scale.</p>
<p>What you do in the first few days after a storm shapes how smoothly the repair goes and how much of it your insurance pays. This guide covers the practical steps. It isn't insurance or legal advice; your policy and your adjuster have the final word.</p>

<h2>First, make it safe and keep animals in</h2>
<p>Stay clear of any fence tangled with power lines or under a hung-up limb. Call the utility for downed lines and wait for a tree crew on anything large.</p>
<p>Once it's safe, the priority on most properties around Magnolia is containment. A missing section means a dog can get out to FM 1774 or horses can get onto a neighbor's place. Temporary fixes are fine: a run of livestock panels or T-posts and wire across a gap, a tarp-wrapped pallet wired into an opening, or a tie-down on a gate that won't latch. Keep receipts for materials. Many policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent more damage, and those costs may be part of the claim.</p>

<h2>Document it before anything gets moved</h2>
<p>Photos taken before cleanup are worth more than anything you can describe later. Take them the same day if you can.</p>
<ul>
<li>Wide shots of the whole fence line from several angles, so the adjuster can see how much of it is affected</li>
<li>Close-ups of snapped or uprooted posts, broken rails, and torn-out pickets</li>
<li>Any tree or limb still lying on the fence, and the stump or break it came from</li>
<li>Gates, operators, and keypads, including any damage to the drive gate on an acreage entrance</li>
<li>A tape measure laid along the damaged stretch, or your own rough footage count</li>
<li>Older photos of the fence, if you have them, to show its condition before the storm</li>
</ul>
<p>Write down the date and time of the storm and when you first saw the damage. If you have a survey or the original fence quote, pull those out too.</p>

<h2>How a homeowners policy usually handles fences</h2>
<p>The Texas Department of Insurance explains that most homeowners policies cover "other structures" such as fences at 10% of the home's insured value. On a $300,000 dwelling limit, that's about $30,000 for everything detached from the house. Wind damage is probably covered if your policy includes wind, and it is usually paid at actual cash value, meaning the depreciated value of an older fence, with your deductible applied. TDI's page on <a href="https://tdi.texas.gov/tips/home-insurance-structures-covered.html" rel="noopener">what home insurance covers for other structures</a> has the details.</p>
<p>Two points come up often on our side of the county. First, homeowners policies don't cover flood, so a fence knocked down by rising water on a low lot or near Lake Conroe usually falls under a separate flood policy, if you have one. Second, the state's coastal windstorm pool doesn't cover Montgomery County, so wind coverage here comes from your regular homeowners policy. If you have questions about your coverage, the TDI help line is 800-252-3439.</p>

<h2>Getting an estimate an adjuster can use</h2>
<p>A one-line number on a business card doesn't help a claim. Ask for a written estimate that breaks the work down so the adjuster can match it against what they saw. Ours list the linear footage being repaired or replaced, the number of posts, the material and height, gates and hardware, tear-out and haul-off of the damaged fence, and any matching needed so the new section looks like the old one.</p>
<p>We'll also separate storm damage from wear that was already there. If part of the fence was leaning before the storm, saying so up front keeps the estimate credible. Adjusters look closely at whether posts snapped or pulled out fresh, or were already rotted at the ground.</p>

<h2>Why a payout can come in lower than the repair</h2>
<p>Depreciation is the big one. A ten-year-old pine fence has less actual cash value than the cost of building a new one, and the deductible comes off on top of that. If the fence damage is small next to your deductible, it may not be worth filing at all.</p>
<p>Partial damage is another. An adjuster may approve replacing only the sections that went down, while the rest of the old fence stays. Pre-existing rot, leaning, or termite damage can reduce what's approved. Ask your adjuster how they handled matching and depreciation if the number looks low; you may be able to submit more documentation.</p>

<h2>Repair, replace, or rebuild stronger</h2>
<p>When a few panels went down on an otherwise sound fence, we repair and match. When the storm exposed rotted posts along the whole run, replacing the fence usually makes more sense, and the claim may cover only part of it. Some owners use the moment to upgrade, such as deeper posts, steel posts behind wood, or a more open style on a wind-exposed lake or golf lot.
