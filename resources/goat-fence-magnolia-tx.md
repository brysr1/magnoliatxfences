<p>A goat fence in Magnolia, TX has a harder job than most farm fences. Cattle mostly respect a line. Goats climb it, lean on it, squeeze through it, stick their heads through it, and stand on anything that slopes. On the acreage tracts around Magnolia, Pinehurst, and Montgomery, we regularly get called out to pastures where the goats came first and the fence was built for something else.</p>
<p>Here's what we plan for when the fence has to hold goats from day one.</p>

<h2>Pick woven wire, not strands</h2>
<p>Barbed wire and plain strand fence hold cattle because a cow won't push between the wires. A goat will. For goats you want woven wire, the kind with vertical stays tied into the horizontal wires so the mesh can't be spread apart.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Height:</strong> plan on at least 4 feet. Some breeds and some individual goats jump, and a taller fence or a strand on top helps with those.</li>
<li><strong>Small openings:</strong> standard field wire has openings big enough for a goat to push its head through. Horned goats get stuck that way, and a goat stuck in a fence on a hot afternoon or a cold night can get hurt or worse. Mesh with small openings, like the roughly 2 by 4 inch no-climb wire we use for horses, solves the problem.</li>
<li><strong>Tight to the ground:</strong> the bottom of the mesh should sit close to the dirt. Kids slip under gaps that an adult goat can't.</li>
</ul>
<p>Small-mesh wire costs more per foot than standard field wire. A lot of owners use it around the barn lot and kidding pens, where goats spend most of their time, and something more economical on outer lines that already have a goat-proof fence inside them.</p>

<h2>Build braces they can't climb</h2>
<p>Goats treat anything angled as a ramp. A diagonal brace on the inside of a corner is a staircase, and we've seen goats walk right up a brace and over the top. When we build corners for goats, we put the bracing on the outside of the pen where that's possible. Where it can't go outside, an H-brace with a level horizontal rail is far harder to climb than a sloped brace. The same goes for anything leaning against the fence: hay feeders, pallets, a woodpile, or a low shed roof near the line.</p>

<h2>Corners and posts in loose sand</h2>
<p>Goats rub and lean on a fence constantly, and tensioned wire pulls on the corners every day. Most of the ground around Magnolia and Pinehurst is sandy loam over a clay subsoil, and loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways. That's why corner and gate posts are the first to lean out here. Once they move, the wire goes slack and the goats find the soft spot within a day.</p>
<p>We set every post in concrete, never gravel, and corner, brace, and gate posts go deeper and wider than line posts, with the pier belled at the bottom so it can't rock. If a hole fills with water, we pump or bail it out before the pour. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting fence posts in Montgomery County soil</a> goes into what we look for when we dig.</p>

<h2>Keeping predators out</h2>
<p>A goat fence works in both directions. Coyotes are common across Montgomery County, and a small goat or a newborn kid is an easy target. Feral hogs can also tear up a weak bottom wire while rooting along a fence line. A few details make a big difference:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A low electric strand:</strong> an offset hot wire on the outside, low to the ground, discourages digging and pushing under the mesh.</li>
<li><strong>An inside offset wire:</strong> a hot wire on the inside at about goat nose height keeps goats from rubbing and leaning on the mesh, which makes the whole fence last longer.</li>
<li><strong>A tight night pen:</strong> many owners build a smaller, stronger pen near the barn for nights and kidding season, and run lighter fencing on the open pasture.</li>
</ul>
<p>We build electric fence on its own or alongside woven wire, so it can be planned into the same job.</p>

<h2>Gates goats can't beat</h2>
<p>Goats are good at latches. A simple gravity latch or a chain hooked over a nail won't last long. Use a latch that takes two motions to open, keep the gap under the gate small enough that a kid can't crawl out, and avoid gate styles with diagonal bracing on the goat side, for the same reason as the corners. Plan gate width around the truck, trailer, or tractor that has to come through, and put gates where you can move animals without cornering them.</p>

<h2>Check the rules first</h2>
<p>Before you buy goats or fence for them, check your deed restrictions or POA rules. Plenty of acreage communities around Magnolia allow livestock, but many subdivisions don't, and some limit the number of animals or how close a pen can be to a property line. If the property sits inside city limits, check the city's rules too.</p>
