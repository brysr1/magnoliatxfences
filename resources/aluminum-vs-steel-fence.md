<p>Walk through Bentwater, April Sound, or Walden and you'll see a lot of black ornamental fence along the water and the fairways. Some of it is aluminum and some is steel, and from the cart path you usually can't tell which. Both are powder-coated, both come in the same picket and rail styles, and both keep the view open, which is why lake and golf communities lean on them.</p>
<p>The choice between them comes down to where the fence goes and what it has to do. A pool fence twenty feet from the lake has different needs than a drive gate at the end of a gravel road off FM 1488. Here's how we help owners sort it out.</p>

<h2>Same style, different metal underneath</h2>
<p>Aluminum is light and doesn't rust. That makes panels easy to handle, easy to rack along a sloped yard, and forgiving when the coating gets scratched. The tradeoff is stiffness. Standard residential aluminum flexes more than steel if something leans on it or hits it.</p>
<p>Steel is heavier and much more rigid. It holds up to a falling limb, a large dog jumping against it, or a bump from a mower or truck far better than aluminum. Its weakness is rust. Steel depends entirely on its coating, and wherever that coating chips down to bare metal, rust can start.</p>

<h2>Rust, water, and pool chemicals</h2>
<p>On lakefront lots along the west side of Lake Conroe, fences live in damp air, sprinkler overspray, and splash. Around a pool they also see chlorinated water every day. Aluminum handles all of that without rusting, which is why it's our usual recommendation for pool enclosures and runs close to the shoreline.</p>
<p>Powder-coated steel can work in those spots too, but it needs watching. A string trimmer nicking the bottom of a post, a chair dragged along the rail, or a pressure washer held too close can open the coating. Clean the spot, let it dry, and touch it up with a matching metal paint. Caught early, it takes a few minutes. Left alone through a wet winter, it turns into a rust streak running down the picket.</p>
<p>One thing to know about aluminum: it won't rust, but it can still pit or chalk where the finish is damaged and left exposed for a long time. A yearly rinse and a look at the bottom of the posts keeps either metal in good shape.</p>

<h2>Where steel earns the extra weight</h2>
<p>Steel is the stronger choice anywhere the fence takes force. Drive gates are the clearest example. A wide gate at an acreage entrance or a gated driveway hangs a lot of weight from one post, and if it carries an automatic opener, the frame has to stay rigid through thousands of cycles. Steel frames hold their shape far better than aluminum at that size.</p>
<p>Steel also makes sense for front-property fencing where security matters, for runs along a road or parking area where a vehicle might bump it, and for yards with big dogs that hit the fence at a run. If you're weighing ornamental metal for security, our guide to <a href="/resources/security-fence-types/">security fence types</a> compares it with chain link and no-climb mesh.</p>

<h2>Pool barriers on lake and golf lots</h2>
<p>Pool fences are where most of our aluminum goes. Most local codes call for a barrier four feet tall or more, picket spacing tight enough to stop a toddler, and gates that swing shut and latch by themselves. The specifics come from your city or the county, whichever code edition applies, and your HOA, so we confirm them for your address before we build. Many lake and golf communities also have their own pool-enclosure policies.</p>
<p>Either metal can meet those rules. Aluminum is usually the better pick because the fence sits in splash range and the self-closing gate hardware gets heavy use. See our <a href="/fence-styles/pool-enclosure/">pool enclosure</a> page for more on gate hardware.</p>

<h2>Using both on one property</h2>
<p>Many of the projects we build use both. A common setup on a lake lot is aluminum around the pool and along the water, with a steel drive gate and steel panels at the street where strength matters more. On acreage, steel often handles the entry gate and the front of the property, aluminum goes around the pool near the house, and ranch fencing covers the rest of the land.</p>
<p>The two can be matched in color and style closely enough that the change is hard to spot. Before mixing them in an HOA neighborhood, check whether the guidelines name a specific material or just a look. Bring the spec sheet for each to your architectural committee if they ask.</p>
