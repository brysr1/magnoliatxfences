<p>How wide should a fence gate be? Wide enough for the biggest thing you'll ever need to get through it, plus some room for your knuckles. That sounds obvious, but around Magnolia and Pinehurst we replace a lot of gates that were sized for a person when the owner needed to get a riding mower, a utility trailer, or a boat through.</p>
<p>Fixing it later means moving a post, so it's worth ten minutes with a tape measure before the fence goes in.</p>

<h2>Walk gates: 4 feet is the comfortable minimum</h2>
<p>A 3-foot gate passes a person fine but gets tight fast with a rolling trash cart or a wheelbarrow. On most subdivision lots we build side yard walk gates at about 4 feet, which handles daily traffic without making the gate heavy.</p>
<p>On a pool barrier, how the gate behaves matters more than width: it has to swing away from the pool, close on its own, and latch every time.</p>

<h2>Mower gates: measure the deck, then add a foot</h2>
<p>This is the gate people undersize most. Walk your riding mower or zero-turn to the garage, measure the deck at its widest point with the side chute down, and add roughly a foot so you aren't threading a needle every Saturday. If a lawn service mows for you, ask what they bring. Commercial mowers often run wider decks than homeowner models.</p>
<p>Once a mower gate gets past about 5 feet, a single wood leaf carries a lot of weight out at the end of its hinges. That's where sag starts. The usual answers are a double gate with two narrower leaves, or a gate built on a welded steel frame. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fix-sagging-fence-gate/">why fence gates sag</a> explains what happens when a wide leaf is hung on light hardware.</p>

<h2>Drive gates for trucks, trailers, and boats</h2>
<p>Texas limits most vehicles and trailers on the road to 8&frac12; feet wide, so a lot of utility, cargo, and boat trailers are built close to that. The trailer's width is only part of it, though. You rarely pull straight in. You're turning off a narrow street or a county road, the trailer cuts inside the truck's path, and side mirrors stick out past both.</p>

<p>For a boat you're towing to Lake Conroe every weekend, back the rig through the planned opening with cones before anyone digs. Clip a cone at 12 feet and you'll clip the post too.</p>

<h3>Acreage entrances</h3>
<p>On acreage, the entrance gate sees hay deliveries, livestock trailers, and equipment, so go wider than you think you need. Set it far enough off the road that your whole rig is out of the lane while it opens.</p>

<h2>Plan the swing, not just the opening</h2>
<p>A gate needs clear, fairly level ground for its full swing. A 6-foot leaf needs 6 feet of open space to swing through, and a double drive gate needs that on both sides. Watch for a sloped drive, a planting bed, a tree root, or a car usually parked right there. Drive gates almost always swing in toward the property, not out toward the road.</p>
<p>Double gates also need a center stop. A drop rod into a sleeve in the drive keeps both leaves from blowing past each other and holds the latch in line. If there's no room to swing at all, a slide gate that rolls along the fence line is the other option.</p>

<h2>Wider gates need stronger posts in sandy ground</h2>
<p>The wider the gate, the more leverage it puts on the hinge post. In the Upland Sand country around Magnolia, Pinehurst, and Montgomery, loose sand on top doesn't grip a post sideways, so gate posts are the first thing to lean when they're set like line posts. We set every post in concrete and give gate posts deeper, wider footings, belled at the base, sized to the gate they carry. If the hole takes on water, it gets pumped out before the pour. You can read more in our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting fence posts in Montgomery County soil</a>.</p>
<p>Wide solid gates also catch wind, so we size hinges and framing for storm load, not just weight. Material and frame options are on our <a href="/fence-styles/gates/">gates and gate openers</a> page.</p>

<h2>A quick checklist before you decide</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>List what goes through.</strong> Mower, trailer, boat, tractor, and anything you might buy in the next few years.</li>
<li><strong>Test the turn.</strong> Back through a coned-off opening if a trailer is involved.</li>
<li><strong>Walk the swing path.</strong> Check grade, beds, and where cars park.</li>
<li><strong>Check your HOA.</strong> Street-facing gates are usually reviewed with the rest of the fence.</li>
</ul>
