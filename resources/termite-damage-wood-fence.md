<p>Most termite damage on a fence is found by accident. Someone leans on a post to pull weeds and it gives, or a mower bumps a picket and the bottom crumbles away. By then the termites have usually been working on that wood for a long time, eating from the inside where the ground keeps it damp.</p>
<p>Native subterranean termites live in soil across Texas, and the wooded lots, mulch beds, and pine stumps common around Magnolia and Pinehurst give them plenty to work with. A wood fence with posts in the ground and boards near the dirt is an easy meal. The good news is that the damage is usually concentrated low on the fence, and much of it can be cut out and replaced.</p>

<h2>Signs your fence has termites</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mud tubes.</strong> Thin, pencil-width tunnels of soil running up the face of a post or along the rot board. Termites build them to stay moist while they travel.</li>
<li><strong>Hollow or papery sound.</strong> Tap along the bottom of posts and pickets with a screwdriver handle. Sound wood clicks; eaten wood thuds or sounds empty.</li>
<li><strong>Layered tunnels inside the wood.</strong> Break off a damaged piece and you'll see galleries running with the grain, often packed with a gritty, mud-colored material.</li>
<li><strong>Soft spots that a screwdriver sinks into</strong> near ground level, especially on posts and the rot board.</li>
<li><strong>Swarmers or shed wings</strong> near the fence line or outdoor lights in spring and early summer.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Termites, rot, or both</h2>
<p>Wood rot and termite damage show up in the same places, and they often travel together. Termites like wood that is already damp and softening, and rot moves faster once termites have opened the wood up.</p>
<p>Rot looks stringy or spongy and is usually darker than the surrounding wood. It may crack into small cubes as it dries. Termite damage looks more like carved channels, with clean wood between the tunnels and that gritty packing inside. If you find mud tubes or live insects, it's termites. If the wood is just soft and dark with no tunnels, it's more likely rot. Either way, the fix for the fence is similar, but termites mean you should also have a pest company look at the house.</p>

<h2>Where to check on Magnolia-area properties</h2>
<p>Start with the bottom foot of the fence. Posts at grade, the rot board, and the lowest few inches of the pickets are where termites get in. Then pay extra attention to these spots:</p>
<p>Stretches that run beside mulch beds or flower beds, where soil and moisture sit against the wood. Sections hit by sprinklers every day. Runs along the edge of a wooded lot, where leaf litter piles up and old pine stumps from lot clearing are still rotting in the ground. Anywhere firewood is stacked against the fence. Gate posts, which are often wood even when the line posts aren't. On acreage, wood split rail and pasture posts along a creek or low area get hit hard because the ground stays wet longer.</p>

<h2>About Formosan termites</h2>
<p>Formosan subterranean termites are an invasive species that forms very large colonies and does damage faster than native termites. Texas A&amp;M's urban entomology program lists them as established in Harris County and in neighboring Liberty and Polk counties. Montgomery County is not on that list as of the page's last update, so we don't tell customers they have Formosan termites. If you're close to the Harris County line or see a big nighttime swarm around porch lights in late May or early June, mention it to your pest control company. <a href="https://urbanentomology.tamu.edu/urban-pests/termites/formosan/" rel="noopener">Texas A&amp;M's Formosan termite page</a> has the current county list.</p>

<h2>Repair or replace</h2>
<p>When the termites only got into a handful of pickets, a rot board, or a post or two, we can usually cut it out and replace it to match. That works well on a fence that is otherwise straight and sound. We'll also look at what drew the termites there in the first place, such as a sprinkler head, a bed built against the fence, or a downspout soaking the base, so the new wood doesn't go the same way.</p>
<p>When the damage runs through several posts, multiple sections, or the rails, replacing the fence makes more sense. Patching a fence that is eaten along the whole line tends to turn into a new repair every few months. We'll walk it with you and give you both prices if it's close.</p>

<h2>Building back with less for termites to eat</h2>
<p>A few choices make a wood fence much less inviting. A rot board along the bottom keeps the pickets off the dirt, and it can be replaced alone if it gets hit; we include one on every wood fence unless you ask us not to. Keeping soil and mulch a few inches below the rot board lets the wood dry out. Cedar resists insects better than untreated pine, and steel posts behind a wood fence take the most vulnerable part out of the ground entirely.</p>
<p>If you'd rather not deal with it again, <a href="/materials/vinyl/">vinyl</a>, <a href="/materials/composite-trex/">composite</a>, and metal fences give termites nothing to eat. Our <a href="/resources/composite-vs-wood-fence/">composite vs. wood comparison</a> walks through the cost difference.</p>

<h2>Who handles the termites themselves</h2>
<p>We fix fences; we don't treat termites. If you find active termites, call a licensed pest control company to inspect and treat, especially if the fence is close to the house, a deck, or a shed. It makes sense to schedule the treatment before or around the fence repair so new wood isn't going into an active colony.</p>
<p>Once the treatment plan is set, send us photos of the damage or book a free estimate. We'll show you which parts can be saved and put the repair or replacement in a written quote.</p>
