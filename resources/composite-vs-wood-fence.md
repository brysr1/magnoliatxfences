<p>We get this question most from homeowners who have already replaced one wood fence and don't want to do it again. They're tired of restaining every few years and watching the bottoms of the pickets go soft. Composite looks like the way out, and for some properties it is. For others, a well-built cedar fence is the better use of the money.</p>
<p>Below is how the two compare around Magnolia, Pinehurst, and the west side of Lake Conroe, using the prices from our own <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a> and what we see when we go back to fences years after they were built.</p>

<p>Those are ballpark ranges, not quotes. Height, gates, slope, and tear-out all move the number. Still, the gap is real: per foot, composite starts at roughly double the top of the cedar range.</p>

<h2>The years after install</h2>
<p>A wood fence needs attention to reach its full life here. Most cedar and pine fences in our area want cleaning and a fresh coat of stain roughly every two to four years, and a fence facing the afternoon sun needs it sooner. Along the way you'll replace a few pickets, maybe a rot board, and keep sprinklers and mulch off the base. Skip that care and a wood fence wears out much sooner.</p>
<p>Composite skips almost all of it. It doesn't need stain or sealer, it won't rot, and termites ignore it. A wash now and then takes off pollen and the mildew that grows on shady sides. Metal, vinyl, and composite fences often last 20 years or more with that kind of light care.</p>
<p>So the comparison isn't just install price. It's install price plus what you'd spend keeping wood looking good over the years you plan to own the house. If you're staying ten years or more and would pay someone to stain, the gap narrows. If you might sell in three or four years, wood almost always wins on cost. Our <a href="/faq/how-long-does-a-fence-last/">fence lifespan page</a> has more on what shortens or stretches each material's life.</p>

<h2>How each one handles our heat and humidity</h2>
<p>Wood moves with the weather. Long, wet stretches swell the boards, and a dry August pulls them back, which leads to cupping, checking, and nails working loose. Untreated wood grays within a season or two in full sun. A rot board and good stain slow all of that, and cedar holds up better than pine on its own.</p>
<p>Composite doesn't swell or rot, but it has its own habits. Colors can lighten a bit during the first months in strong sun before they settle. Darker boards get warm in midsummer. A composite board weighs more than a cedar one, which means the posts and rails carrying it need to be sized up, especially in loose sand. It also presents a solid face to the wind, so the footings matter as much as the boards.</p>

<h2>Getting it past the HOA</h2>
<p>Cedar privacy fencing is the standard in most deed-restricted neighborhoods around Magnolia and Pinehurst, and it usually clears review without much discussion. Builder fences in many subdivisions are wood, and replacing one with matching wood is the easiest approval you'll get.</p>
<p>Composite is newer, and some HOA guidelines simply don't mention it. That can go either way. Some committees approve it readily if the color is close to the approved stain, while others ask for a sample or treat it as a variance. If you're leaning toward composite, send the committee the manufacturer, product line, and color name up front. Our <a href="/hoa-fencing/">HOA fencing</a> page explains how we help with the application.</p>

<h2>Where each one fits on a bigger lot</h2>
<p>On acreage, composite is rarely the boundary fence. Running it along a multi-acre property line costs too much for a fence few people will see. Where it shines is close to the house: around a pool, a patio, or an outdoor kitchen where you'll look at it every day. Wood privacy near the house and ranch fencing beyond is still the most common setup on larger tracts.</p>
<p>On lake and golf lots in communities like Bentwater, April Sound, and Walden, the water or fairway side usually calls for open ornamental metal. Privacy, if allowed, goes between homes. Those side runs are short, which makes composite easier to justify there than on a long subdivision backyard.</p>

<h2>A quick way to decide</h2>
<p>Composite tends to be the right call if you plan to stay a long time, would rather never stain again, and the run is short enough that the per-foot price doesn't sting. Wood tends to be the right call if the budget is firm, the run is long, you might sell in a few years, or your HOA has a cedar spec it doesn't like to bend on. Plenty of owners mix them, with composite on the run they see from the back porch and cedar everywhere else.</p>
