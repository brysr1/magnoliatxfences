<p>Around Magnolia, fall is the break between the long wet stretch of summer and the cold fronts that roll through after Thanksgiving. Hurricane season officially runs through November 30, but by mid-October most yards dry out enough to get a good look at the fence. Posts that shifted during summer rain show it now, and boards that stayed damp for months start to show the dark edges that come before rot.</p>
<p>This checklist is built for the kinds of property we work on most: subdivision backyards in Magnolia and Pinehurst, multi-acre tracts off FM 1774 and FM 1488, and waterfront and fairway homes around Bentwater, April Sound, and Walden. Most of it takes an afternoon and a few hand tools.</p>

<h2>Walk the whole line, not just the part you see from the patio</h2>
<p>Start at a corner and walk every foot of the fence, both sides if you can reach them. The stretch behind the shed or along a wooded back line is where problems hide longest. Photograph anything that looks off; the pictures help if you call us and give you a baseline if a storm hits later.</p>
<p>Here is what to look and feel for as you go:</p>
<ul>
<li>Posts that rock when you push on them at chest height, or that are visibly out of plumb</li>
<li>Pickets with dark, soft bottoms or nails backing out of the rails</li>
<li>Rails pulling away from posts, or brackets that have rusted through</li>
<li>Gaps under the fence where dogs have started digging or water has cut a channel</li>
<li>Gates that drag, need a lift to latch, or swing shut on their own when they didn't before</li>
<li>Vines, wisteria, or young trees growing into the fence line</li>
<li>Limbs hanging over the fence that could come down in the next front</li>
</ul>

<h2>Get the pine straw and leaf litter off the boards</h2>
<p>Lots around Magnolia and Pinehurst tend to have plenty of pines and oaks, and by fall the base of the fence collects a mat of needles and leaves. That layer holds moisture against the wood for days after every rain. The bottom few inches of the pickets and the rot board take the worst of it.</p>
<p>Rake the debris back at least a few inches from the fence. Do the same with mulch beds and soil that has crept up over the years; ideally the dirt line stays below the bottom of the rot board. If a flower bed was built right against the fence, it's worth pulling the edging back a little so the boards can dry out between waterings.</p>

<h2>Aim the sprinklers somewhere else</h2>
<p>One of the most common repair calls we get is a section of fence that rotted out years ahead of the rest. Almost every time, a sprinkler head was hitting the same boards every morning. Fall is a good time to run each irrigation zone and watch where the water lands.</p>
<p>Turn heads so they spray toward the lawn, swap a rotor for a lower-arc nozzle, or move the head a foot off the fence. Make a note of where irrigation lines run along the fence, since 811 locators don't mark private sprinkler pipe and we'll want to know before we dig near it.</p>

<h2>Use the drier weeks to clean and stain wood</h2>
<p>Stain and sealer need dry wood going in and a few dry days after. Summer here rarely gives you that, with afternoon storms and heavy humidity. Fall usually does. Mild temperatures also slow the finish down enough that it soaks into the grain instead of flashing off the surface on a hot board.</p>
<p>Wash the fence first to take off mildew and the green film that builds up on shady sides. Then let it dry fully before you put anything on, which can take longer than people expect on the north face of a fence. If you live in a deed-restricted neighborhood, check that your stain color matches what the HOA allows. Our <a href="/fence-staining-power-washing/">staining and power washing</a> page explains the difference between semi-transparent and solid stains.</p>

<h2>Acreage: check wire tension, braces, and gates</h2>
<p>On larger tracts, fall is when we see owners finally get around to the back pasture. Walk any wire fence and look at the corner and brace assemblies first, because a brace that has started to lean lets the whole run go slack. Check for limbs on the wire, staples pulled out of wood posts, and spots where hogs or dogs have pushed under.</p>
<p>Swing every pipe or drive gate. If a wide gate has started to drop at the latch end, deal with it before winter rain softens the ground around the hinge post. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fix-sagging-fence-gate/">fixing a sagging gate</a> covers how to tell whether it's the hinges, the frame, or the post. If you have an automatic opener, clear the photo eyes and solar panel of pollen and needles.</p>

<h2>Lake and golf lots: rinse metal and test pool gates</h2>
<p>Ornamental aluminum and steel need very little, but fall is a good time to hose off pollen and grass clippings and look closely at the coating. On steel, a chip that reaches bare metal will start to rust if it sits through a wet winter. Clean it, dry it, and touch it up with a matching paint made for metal.</p>
<p>If the fence encloses a pool, open each gate and let go of it from a few different positions. It should swing shut and latch on its own every time. A self-closing hinge that has lost tension or a latch that has drifted out of line is a quick adjustment, and it's one you want working before anyone forgets to check it.</p>

<h2>What to fix now and what can wait</h2>
<p>A few loose pickets or a gate that needs a hinge screw can wait until spring if you're short on time. A post that rocks, a section that leans, or a limb resting on the fence should not, because winter fronts bring gusty north wind and the next storm season starts June 1. A leaning post in sandy ground only gets worse; see <a href="/resources/fix-leaning-fence-post-sandy-soil/">how to fix a leaning post</a> for the warning signs.</p>
