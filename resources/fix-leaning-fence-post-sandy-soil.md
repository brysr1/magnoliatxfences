<p>Upland ground around Magnolia, Pinehurst, and Montgomery is mostly sandy on top, with a slower-draining clay layer underneath. The sand is easy to dig and easy to set a post in. It's also easy for that post to move later if the hole was shallow, the concrete was skimpy, or water keeps finding its way to the footing.</p>
<p>Before you grab a shovel, take ten minutes to figure out why the post is leaning. The fix for a rotted post is different from the fix for one that was never set deep enough, and a brace that works for one will fail on the other.</p>

<h2>Start with three quick checks</h2>
<p>First, count. Walk the line and note every post that is out of plumb. One post on its own usually points to something local, like a downspout, a tree, or a low spot. Several posts leaning the same direction along a run point to the way the whole fence was built.</p>
<p>Second, look at the direction. A fence that leans away from a neighbor's yard after a storm took wind on one face. A post leaning toward a low corner of the lot is often sitting in ground that stays wet.</p>
<p>Third, push on it. Grab the post at shoulder height and rock it. If it pivots at the ground line and you feel the wood flex or crunch, rot is the likely cause. If the whole footing moves in the soil like a loose tooth, the post was set too shallow or the ground around it has softened.</p>

<h2>What we usually find when we dig one out</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Rot at grade.</strong> Wood posts fail right where soil, water, and air meet. Mulch or dirt piled against the post, or a concrete collar that holds water against the wood, speeds this up.</li>
<li><strong>A shallow or thin footing.</strong> Builder fences and quick installs sometimes go in with a short hole and a bag or less of concrete. In loose sand that gives the post very little to push against.</li>
<li><strong>Washout or soft ground.</strong> A downspout, a drainage path, or a sprinkler line leaking near the post can carry sand away or keep the bottom of the hole saturated. On lots where water perches on the clay layer after a heavy rain, the footing can sit in wet ground for days.</li>
<li><strong>Roots and trees.</strong> A growing oak or pine near the line can push a footing over from below, and a big limb landing on the fence can crack a post that otherwise looked fine.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Brace it, reset it, or replace it</h2>
<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>What you find</th><th>Usual fix</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Solid post, footing moves in loose soil</td><td>Pull it, dig deeper and wider, and set it again in fresh concrete</td></tr>
<tr><td>Post rotted at or below ground</td><td>Replace the post; a brace only hides the problem</td></tr>

</tbody>

<p>A diagonal 2x4 staked to the ground is fine to hold a post upright for a week or two until it can be fixed. It's not a repair. In sandy soil the stake works loose, and the post will be back where it started after the next soaking rain.</p>

<h2>Leaning posts on acreage fences</h2>
<p>On wire fences across pasture or a property line, a single line post leaning a little is rarely urgent. The corner and brace assemblies are what matter. If an H-brace has started to tip, the tension in the wire pulls the whole run slack, and you'll see sagging wire for a long way down the line.</p>
<p>Look at the brace wire or rail first, then the corner post itself. A corner that moved after a wet spell often needs a deeper post and a longer brace. If you're replacing a long run anyway, our <a href="/acreage-ranch-fencing/">acreage and ranch fencing</a> page explains how we lay out corners and gates from the start.</p>

<h2>What a homeowner can do safely</h2>
<p>Pull mulch and soil back from the base of the post so it can dry. Point downspouts and sprinkler heads away from it; our <a href="/resources/fall-fence-maintenance-magnolia-tx/">fall maintenance checklist</a> covers the rest of the fence line. If a panel is loose rather than the post, a few new exterior screws through the rail into the post may be all it needs.</p>
<p>Digging out and resetting a single mid-line post is a reasonable weekend job if you're handy. Fence work is one of the projects <a href="https://texas811.org/homeowner/" rel="noopener">Texas 811</a> tells homeowners to call about before digging, so put in a locate request first. Remember that sprinkler lines, landscape lighting, and lines out to a shop or well house are private and won't be marked. A gate post, a corner, a post on a shared line, or more than one or two posts is where we'd suggest calling us instead.</p>

<h2>How we reset a post so it stays put</h2>
<p>We set every wood fence post at least 24 inches deep with 80 pounds of concrete, and the footing gets deeper or wider wherever the hole turns up loose sand or water. Gate and corner posts get more than that because they carry the most load. If the hole fills with water, it gets pumped or bailed before concrete goes in, since concrete mixed into standing water cures weak.</p>
