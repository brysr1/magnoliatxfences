<p>Whether to repair or replace a fence is the question behind most of the service calls we get around Magnolia and Pinehurst. A limb came down in a storm, a gate won't latch, or a run of pickets has gone gray and soft at the bottom. Sometimes a few hundred dollars of repair buys years. Sometimes it buys a few months before the next section goes.</p>
<p>Here's how we sort it out when we walk a fence, so you can make a rough call before anyone shows up with a tape measure.</p>

<h2>Start with the posts, not the pickets</h2>
<p>Pickets and rails are the cheap, replaceable part of a wood fence. Posts are the frame everything hangs on, and they decide most repair-or-replace calls.</p>
<p>Push on a few posts near the middle of each run and at every corner and gate. Then dig an inch or two around the base of one and press a screwdriver into the wood at the soil line. If it sinks in easily, that post is rotting where you can't see it. One soft or leaning post is a repair. Soft posts all down the line mean the fence is near the end, even if the boards still look fine.</p>
<p>Around here, the gate and corner posts usually move first. Most lots in this part of Montgomery County have sandy loam on top, and loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways, so the posts carrying the most load start to lean before the line posts do. Our page on <a href="/faq/why-fence-posts-fail/">why fence posts fail</a> walks through the usual causes.</p>

<h2>How much of the fence is damaged</h2>
<p>A storm-damaged section on a fence that is otherwise straight and solid is an easy repair. We set new posts in concrete, rebuild the section, and match the boards as closely as we can.</p>
<p>The math changes when the damage is spread out. As a rough guide, once a third or more of the fence needs new posts, rails, or pickets, you're paying repair labor on most of the line and still keeping the old parts that are about to fail next. At that point the two prices get close, and the new fence comes with a fresh start and a workmanship warranty on the whole run.</p>

<h2>Age and material</h2>
<p>A wood fence here often lasts somewhere around 10 to 20 years, with bare pressure-treated pine toward the short end and cedar that's been stained on schedule toward the long end. Our <a href="/faq/how-long-does-a-fence-last/">fence lifespan guide</a> has ranges for other materials.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A young fence with damage:</strong> almost always worth repairing. The rest of it has years left.</li>
<li><strong>A fence in its last few years:</strong> repair only what's needed to keep it standing and secure, and start planning the replacement.</li>
<li><strong>Vinyl, aluminum, and steel:</strong> usually repairable by the panel or section, as long as the matching parts are still available and the posts are sound.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Matching new boards to an old fence</h2>
<p>New wood next to a fence that has weathered for eight years will stand out. Cedar and pine both gray over time, and a stain can help a repaired section blend in, but it rarely disappears completely. If the repair faces the street or your HOA is strict about appearance, that matters more than it would on a back property line behind the pines.</p>

<h2>When repair is the right call</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>The damage is in one area:</strong> a fallen limb, a car backing into a corner, or a single storm-blown section.</li>
<li><strong>Most posts are solid and plumb:</strong> one or two loose posts can be reset or replaced.</li>
<li><strong>The gate is the problem:</strong> sagging gates often need new hardware, a brace, or a rebuilt gate post rather than a new fence.</li>
<li><strong>The fence is less than halfway through its expected life.</strong></li>
</ul>

<h2>When replacing the fence makes more sense</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Posts are soft or leaning along much of the line.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Repairs keep coming back:</strong> if you've fixed different sections two or three times in a couple of years, the fence is telling you something.</li>
<li><strong>You want a different fence anyway:</strong> taller, a different material, or a wider gate for a trailer or mower.</li>
<li><strong>The fence no longer does its job:</strong> gaps a dog can get through, or a pool fence with a gate that won't self-close.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Repair or replace fence costs</h2>
<p>A repair is priced on the work involved: how many posts, how many feet of rails and pickets, and whether a gate needs rebuilding. A replacement adds tear-out and haul-off of the old fence on top of the new installation. Our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a> lists ballpark per-foot ranges by material.</p>
<p>One more thing to weigh: if we hang new pickets on old posts and rails we didn't build, our workmanship warranty doesn't cover that structure. On a full replacement, it covers installation defects on the whole run we built.</p>
