<p>A good horse fence in Magnolia, TX has to do more than mark a line. Horses lean on it, reach over it, rub on it, and run at it when something spooks them. On the acreage tracts west of town, out toward Pinehurst and Montgomery, and along the west side of Lake Conroe, we see plenty of pastures where the fence was built for cattle or just for a boundary, then asked to hold horses later.</p>
<p>These are the six details we look at first on any horse pasture or paddock we walk.</p>

<h2>The horse fence checklist for Magnolia acreage</h2>

<h3>1. Height that matches the horse</h3>
<p>Most horse fencing runs somewhere around 4.5 to 5 feet tall, and taller for a horse that has already shown it will jump or lean over. A good rule of thumb is a fence top close to the height of the horse's withers. A 4-foot cattle fence is an invitation for a horse to reach over for the grass on the other side, and that constant pressure is what bends the top wire and loosens the posts.</p>

<h3>2. Openings a hoof can't get through</h3>
<p>This is the biggest difference between horse fence and general pasture wire. Standard field wire has openings large enough for a hoof to slip through when a horse paws or rolls near the line, and a hoof caught in wire is how legs get cut up. No-climb woven wire, with roughly 2 by 4 inch openings, keeps hooves out of the mesh. It costs more than field wire, so many owners use it on paddocks and smaller pastures where horses spend the most time, and something more economical where they rarely go.</p>

<h3>3. No barbed wire where horses live</h3>
<p>Barbed wire is still common on cattle land around Montgomery County, and it does that job fine. It's a poor choice for horses. They run into fences more than cattle do, and barbs turn a bump into a vet call. If a horse pasture shares a line with an old barbed fence, plan on replacing that stretch or setting a horse-safe fence inside it.</p>

<h3>4. A top line horses can see</h3>
<p>Wire mesh nearly disappears from a distance, which is nice from the road and less nice for a horse at a gallop. A wood board or pipe rail along the top gives them a line they can see, and it keeps the top of the mesh from being pushed down by a horse leaning over. Wood boards look the part along road frontage but will get chewed by some horses, so a pipe top rail is the tougher option for busy paddocks. Our <a href="/materials/farm-ranch/">farm and ranch materials</a> page compares wire, rail, and pipe in more detail.</p>

<h3>5. Corners and braces built for loose sand</h3>
<p>A wire fence is only as tight as its corners. Most of the ground around Magnolia and Pinehurst is sandy loam on top of a clay subsoil, and loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways. The constant pull of tensioned wire, plus a horse leaning on the line, shows up first as a corner or gate post that starts to tip. Once it moves, the whole run goes slack.</p>
<p>That's why we build H-brace corner assemblies and set every post in concrete, never gravel. Corner, brace, and gate posts go deeper and wider than line posts, with piers belled at the base so they resist being pulled over. If a hole fills with water, we pump or bail it out before the pour. Our guide to <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">fence posts in Montgomery County soil</a> explains what we look for when we dig.</p>

<h3>6. Gates and a clear fence line</h3>
<p>Plan gates around the equipment, not just the horse. A truck and trailer, a tractor with a shredder, or a hay delivery all need a wider opening than people expect, and a gate in a corner makes it hard to move horses through. Horses also lean on and rub against gates, so a gate post gets the heaviest footing on the whole fence and a latch a curious horse can't work open. If you might add an automatic opener later, say so up front, since it changes how the gate post and swing should be planned.</p>
<p>Before any of it goes in, the line needs clearing. In pine country, limbs and whole trees come down in storms, and a fence built tight against a tree line will get hit. Clearing brush and small trees back from the line makes the fence easier to inspect and much easier to repair.</p>

<h2>Where to start</h2>
<p>Most horse properties we fence around Magnolia end up as a mix: no-climb with a top rail around the paddocks, a more economical wire across the back pasture, and rail fencing or wood along the road where looks matter. Walking the property first, with the corners and gates marked, is what keeps that mix from turning into a patchwork.
