<h2>Fencing for land, not just lots</h2>
<p>Head a few miles off FM 1774 or FM 149 and the lots turn into acreage &mdash; two, five, ten acres or more in unincorporated Montgomery County, often with no HOA at all. Fencing that kind of property is a different job. Runs are long, ground isn't level, tree lines and creeks get in the way, and the fence may need to hold a horse, a goat, or a determined dog rather than just mark a boundary.</p>
<p>We plan acreage fences from the corners out. Strong corner and brace assemblies are what keep wire tight for years, so that's where we start. Then we choose the fence type for each stretch: no-climb along a horse pasture, field wire around goats, rail fencing along the road frontage where looks matter, and wide gates wherever a truck, trailer, or tractor needs through.</p>
</div>
</section>

<h2>Acreage fence options</h2>
<div class="cards76">
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>Barbed wire</h3><p>The traditional cattle fence and the most economical per foot, along with field wire. It does that job well, but it's a poor choice anywhere horses live.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>Field wire</h3><p>Woven wire for goats, sheep, dogs, and general pasture use. It nearly disappears from a distance, which suits long back lines.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>No-climb horse fence</h3><p>Tight mesh, roughly 2 by 4 inch openings, that keeps hooves out of the wire. Horse fence usually runs about 4.5 to 5 feet tall, often with a board or pipe top rail horses can see.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>Pipe fence</h3><p>Welded pipe is strong, clean, and long-lasting for pens, arenas, and entries. Painted pipe may need touch-ups over time to keep rust away.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>Split rail &amp; board</h3><p>Classic rail fencing for road frontage, driveways, and front pastures. Some horses chew wood boards, so pipe is the tougher choice for busy paddocks.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>Electric fence</h3><p>We build electric fence for pastures and acreage, on its own or alongside other fence types.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>Property-line fencing</h3><p>Barbed wire, field wire, or privacy fencing to clearly mark your boundary.</p></div></div>
</div>

<h2>Gates and openers</h2>
<p>Plan gates around the equipment, not just the opening. A truck and trailer, a tractor with a shredder, or a hay delivery all need more room than people expect, and a gate tucked in a corner makes it hard to move animals through. Gate posts carry the whole gate from one side, so they get the deepest, widest footings on the job.</p>
<p>If you want an automatic opener now or later, say so up front, since it changes how the gate post and swing are planned. On acreage, solar openers are common because running power down a long driveway gets expensive, as long as the panel clears the tree line. Set the gate far enough into the property that your truck and trailer are fully off FM 1774 or the county road while it opens. See our guide to <a href="/resources/automatic-gate-opener-acreage/">gate openers for acreage</a> for solar vs. wired, swing vs. slide, and UL 325 safety basics.</p>

<h2>Montgomery County acreage conditions</h2>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li><strong>Sand over clay.</strong> The most common soil in the county is Conroe loamy fine sand, according to the NRCS soil survey: sand on top, with slower-draining clay and ironstone underneath. Loose sand doesn't grip a post sideways, so braced corners and concrete matter more here. See <a href="/resources/fence-posts-montgomery-county-soil/">setting posts in Montgomery County soil</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Water in the hole.</strong> Rain can sit on top of the clay, and a fresh hole on a low spot may fill within minutes. The water comes out before the concrete goes in.</li>
<li><strong>Creek bottoms and lake lots.</strong> Land along Spring Creek, Lake Creek, and the west side of Lake Conroe brings wetter ground. Montgomery County's floodplain rules require a development permit for work in a mapped floodplain, though a three-strand barbed wire fence is exempt.</li>
<li><strong>Pines and storms.</strong> Limbs and whole trees come down in storms. Clearing brush and small trees back from the line makes the fence easier to inspect and repair.</li>
<li><strong>Deed restrictions.</strong> A farm fence on acreage usually doesn't need a county permit, but many acreage communities have deed restrictions or POA rules. We check yours before we draw anything.</li>
</ul>
