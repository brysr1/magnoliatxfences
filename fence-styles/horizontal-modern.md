<h2>What it looks like</h2>
<p>Horizontal fences run boards or slats side to side instead of up and down. Boards can sit tight together for full privacy or have small, consistent gaps for a lighter, more open look. The result is a clean, contemporary fence that pairs well with newer home designs and outdoor living areas.</p>
<p>Most horizontal fences are five to six feet tall. Because long horizontal boards can sag over wide spans, posts are typically spaced closer together, and some designs add a center support or a top cap to keep the lines straight.</p>
</div>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li><strong>Privacy level:</strong> Full or partial, depending on spacing</li>
<li><strong>Typical height:</strong> 5&ndash;6 ft typical</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section class="section76-alt full-bleed76">
<div class="inner76">
<h2>When a horizontal fence makes sense</h2>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li><strong>Newer and modern homes.</strong> Horizontal boards match flat rooflines, black window trim, and clean elevations.</li>
<li><strong>Outdoor living areas.</strong> A slatted section screens a patio, pool, hot tub, or outdoor kitchen without closing it in.</li>
<li><strong>Smaller lots.</strong> On a compact lot the fence is a big part of the view, and horizontal lines make the yard feel wider.</li>
<li><strong>Feature walls on acreage.</strong> A short horizontal run near the house, with ranch or field fence on the rest of the property.</li>
<li><strong>Check the rules first.</strong> Not every HOA allows horizontal fences, especially facing the street.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</section>

<h2>Acreage or subdivision lot?</h2>
<div class="cards76">
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>On acreage</h3><p>On acreage, a horizontal fence is usually a feature fence around a patio, pool, or outdoor kitchen rather than a property-line fence.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>On subdivision lots</h3><p>On subdivision lots, horizontal fences make a strong statement, but not every HOA allows them. If your property has HOA or deed restrictions, check with your board before you commit to the style.</p></div></div>

<section class="section76">
<div class="prose76">
<h2>Local rules</h2>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li><strong>City of Magnolia.</strong> Inside city limits, a fence needs a city permit: $65 plus a $50 administrative fee on the November 2024 schedule.</li>
<li><strong>City of Montgomery.</strong> Fences are on the city's list of projects that need a permit.</li>
<li><strong>Unincorporated Montgomery County.</strong> A typical residential fence usually doesn't need a county permit, but HOA or POA approval may still apply.</li>
<li><strong>HOAs and POAs.</strong> Many set the fence style, height, and stain color that faces the street. Send us your deed restrictions and we'll check before we draw anything.</li>
</ul>
<p>See <a href="/service-areas/magnolia-tx/">fencing in Magnolia</a> and our <a href="/faq/fence-permits-magnolia-montgomery/">permit guide</a>.</p>

<section class="section76-alt full-bleed76">
<div class="inner76">
<h2>What affects the cost</h2>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li><strong>Material.</strong> On our <a href="/pricing-guide/">pricing guide</a>, cedar typically runs $30&ndash;$47 per linear foot installed and composite $100&ndash;$175+.</li>
<li><strong>Board width and gaps.</strong> Narrow boards and slatted spacing take more pieces and more cuts.</li>
<li><strong>Framing.</strong> Closer post spacing, center supports, and steel posts keep long boards straight.</li>
<li><strong>Height.</strong> Taller fences need more boards and longer posts.</li>
<li><strong>Trim and gates.</strong> Caps, trim, and matching gates add labor.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</section>

<h2>Horizontal fence questions</h2>
<div class="faq76">
<details><summary>Will long horizontal boards sag?</summary><div class="answer76"><p>Not when the framing is right. We space posts closer and add support where the span needs it, so the boards stay straight.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Can a horizontal fence give full privacy?</summary><div class="answer76"><p>Yes. Boards set tight give full privacy. A slatted layout lets light and air through and screens most of the view.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Cedar or composite?</summary><div class="answer76"><p>Cedar costs less and takes stain well. Composite costs more up front, holds straight lines, and skips the staining. See <a href="/materials/composite-trex/">composite</a>.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Does my HOA allow horizontal fences?</summary><div class="answer76"><p>It depends on the neighborhood. Some allow them anywhere, some only in the backyard, and some not at all. We check your deed restrictions before we quote.</p></div></details>
