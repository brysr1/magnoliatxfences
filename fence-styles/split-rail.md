<h2>What it looks like</h2>
<p>Split rail fences use rough-split rails set into mortised posts, usually two or three rails high, for a rustic, open look. Post-and-rail (or board) fencing uses milled boards fastened to square posts for a cleaner, more finished version of the same idea.</p>
<p>Most rail fences are three to four and a half feet tall. They mark a boundary without blocking the view, and they're often paired with welded wire or no-climb mesh on the inside when they also need to hold pets or livestock.</p>
</div>

<h2>Rails and posts</h2>
<p>The rails set the look. Rough-split wood rails give the rustic version. Milled wood boards give a cleaner post-and-rail fence that can be stained or painted to match the house. Vinyl rails stay bright and never need painting, which helps on long frontage with no shade. Welded pipe is the toughest of the group and suits entries and paddocks where horses lean on or chew a wood board.</p>
<p>The posts carry everything. Split rail uses mortised posts that the rails slide into. Board fences fasten to square posts. On wood fences, posts are usually pressure-treated pine, and galvanized steel is an option where rot or termites are a concern. Whatever the post, end and corner posts get deeper, wider footings so a long run stays straight in the sandy ground around Magnolia.</p>
</div>

<h2>On Magnolia acreage and driveways</h2>
<p>Head off FM 1774 or FM 149 onto two, five, or ten acres and rail fencing is what you'll see along the road. It marks the property line and frames the house without hiding the land behind it. Most owners put rail where it's seen, along the frontage, the driveway, and the front pasture, then switch to field wire or barbed wire across the back where cost per foot matters more.</p>
<p>A rail fence along a driveway often ends at a drive gate. Plan the gate for a truck and trailer, and set it far enough in that you're fully off the road while it opens. On a wooded line, split rail bends around trees more naturally than a solid fence, and along a creek bottom it lets water pass. See <a href="/acreage-ranch-fencing/">acreage &amp; ranch fencing</a> for how we lay out a full property.</p>
</div>
<div>
<h2>Wire backing for pets or livestock</h2>
<p>A rail fence on its own won't hold a dog or a goat. If it needs to, we add welded wire or no-climb mesh on the inside of the rails. That keeps the open ranch look from the road while closing the gaps between rails.</p>
<p>For horses, no-climb mesh with roughly 2 by 4 inch openings keeps hooves out of the wire, and a top rail gives them a line they can see. Skip barbed wire anywhere horses live. Our <a href="/materials/farm-ranch/">farm &amp; ranch materials</a> page compares wire, rail, and pipe.</p>
</div>

<h2>Acreage or subdivision lot?</h2>
<div class="cards76">
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>On acreage</h3><p>Rail fencing is built for acreage. It's the most popular choice along road frontage, driveways, and front pastures on the multi-acre lots around Magnolia, often with wire fencing across the back of the property.</p></div></div>
<div class="card76"><div class="card76-body"><h3>On subdivision lots</h3><p>On subdivision lots, a short run of rail fencing can frame a front yard or driveway for a ranch-style look. If your property has HOA or deed restrictions, check with your board before adding a front-yard fence.</p></div></div>

<section class="section76">
<h2>What affects the cost</h2>
<p class="section-intro76">Rail and pipe sit at the premium end of acreage fencing, which is why most owners use them where they're most visible. Your written quote spells out each of these.</p>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li>Total footage, fence height, and how many rails high</li>
<li>Rail material: split wood, milled board, vinyl, or pipe</li>
<li>Post material, and the number of end, corner, and gate posts</li>
<li>Wire or mesh backing, if the fence has to hold animals</li>
<li>Number and width of gates, and any automatic opener</li>
<li>Slope, trees, clearing, and equipment access along the line</li>
<li>Tear-out and haul-off of an old fence</li>
<li>Stain or sealer on wood rails</li>
</ul>
