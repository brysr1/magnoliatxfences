<h2>What's in every wood fence we build</h2>
<p>Cedar and pressure-treated pine fences from 76 Fence of Magnolia include:</p>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li>Three rails instead of two, for strength and fewer warped pickets</li>
<li>A rot board along the bottom (6&Prime; standard; taller if you want it)</li>
<li>Posts set at least 24&Prime; deep with 80&nbsp;lb of concrete per post, and deeper or wider in loose sand or wet ground</li>
<li>Simpson brackets where rails meet posts, instead of toe-nailed joints</li>
<li>Hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails</li>
</ul>
<p>These details are a big part of where our prices start. Rails pulling away from posts is one of the first failures on a cheaply built fence, and nails that back out or corrode leave rust streaks down the boards. Spending a little more up front saves repairs later.</p>

<h2>What affects your price</h2>
<ul class="checklist76">
<li>Total linear footage and fence height</li>
<li>Material grade: wood species, metal and wire gauge, panel style</li>
<li>Number and width of gates, and any automatic openers</li>
<li>Slope, trees and roots, clearing, and equipment access</li>
<li>Tear-out and haul-off of an existing fence</li>
<li>HOA requirements or permits, where they apply</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h2>About the low-ball quote</h2>
<p>If one estimate is far below the others, ask why. Quality lumber, metal, and concrete cost what they cost, and so does a crew that sets posts correctly. A much cheaper bid often means fewer rails, less concrete, thinner material, or a contractor who won't be around when the gate needs adjusting after the posts settle.</p>
<p>Ask every contractor the same questions: how deep are the posts, how much concrete goes in each hole, how are rails attached, and what does the warranty cover? Our <a href="/warranty/">workmanship warranty</a> and <a href="/process/">process</a> pages show our answers.</p>
